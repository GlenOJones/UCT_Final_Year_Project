\chapter{Underwater stereo calibration}
\label{ch:calibration}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\label{sec:ch5.exp1_calibration}
This chapter determines the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of the StellarHD
stereo camera pair, both in air and underwater, as well as the best calibration technique.
The theory of the calibration models compared here is introduced in
\autoref{subsec:ch5.theory_calibration_techniques}.
Three experiments are conducted. The first two optimise AprilGrid detection by
choosing the image channel and tuning the detector parameters. The third
compares five underwater compensation techniques. The in-air calibration acts
as a baseline, showing how the underwater environment changes the calibration.
The experiments examine different things, and each one informs the next stage of testing. For these
reasons, the results of each experiment are analysed separately.

A calibration model describes how the normalised image coordinates are distorted by the lens.
In this way, detected pixel positions can be mapped back to undistorted normalised coordinates
(\autoref{fig:ch3.projection_chain}). Five techniques built on these models are explained here and tested in
\hyperref[subsec:ch5.underwater_compensation_analysis]{\textcolor{blue}{Experiment~3}}.


\subsection{Brown-Conrady}
% Refernce doces: https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/d9/d0c/group__calib3d.html
% Explain the baseic maths
Brown-Conrady is a camera model capable of correcting lens induced geometric 
distortions \cite{xuComprehensiveOverviewFishEye2024}. The model handles radial distortion (barrel and pincushion) by
 multiplying each point by $(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6)$, where $k_i$ are the radial coefficients and $r$ is the distance from the
image centre. Tangential distortion, caused
by the lens not being perfectly lined up with the sensor, is handled by adding an
offset controlled by $p_1$ and $p_2$. Joining together, this gives: 
\begin{align}
x_d &= x\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + 2p_1 x y + p_2\,(r^2 + 2x^2) \label{eq:ch5.bc_x} \\
y_d &= y\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + p_1\,(r^2 + 2y^2) + 2p_2 x y
\end{align}
where $(x, y)$ are the undistorted coordinates, $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, and
$(x_d, y_d)$ are the distorted coordinates.

OpenCV implements this through \href{https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d9/d0c/group__calib3d.html#ga3207604e4b1a1758aa66acb6ed5aa65d}{\textcolor{blue}{\texttt{cv2.calibrateCamera}}},
using Zhang's method \cite{zhangFlexibleNewTechnique2000} for an
initial estimate. This is followed by Levenberg-Marquardt optimisation to refine all parameters \cite{CvCalibrateCameraAuroraVision}.

 % REF https://docs.adaptive-vision.com/studio/filters/CameraCalibrationAnd3DReconstruction/cvCalibrateCamera.html


\subsection{Kannala-Brandt}
% https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/db/d58/group__calib3d__fisheye.html
% Explan the maths basic with references
The Kannala-Brandt distortion model is an extension of the standard Brown-Conrady model, providing a more accurate distortion
representation for wide-angle lenses. Instead of a single pinhole projection model ($x=f\tan\theta$),
 it approximates four different projection models with a polynomial in the angle $\theta$ of the incoming light ray:
\begin{equation}
r(\theta) = k_1\theta + k_2\theta^3 + k_3\theta^5 + k_4\theta^7 + k_5\theta^9
\end{equation}
Because the distortion is written as a function of the ray angle $\theta$ rather
than the image radius, the model can handle wide fields of view. In the pinhole
model the radius $r = f\tan\theta$ grows towards infinity as $\theta$ nears
$90^\circ$, whereas $\theta$ itself stays finite.

OpenCV uses a simplified version in \href{https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/db/d58/group__calib3d__fisheye.html#gad626a78de2b1dae7489e152a5a5a89e1}{\textcolor{blue}{\texttt{cv2.fisheye.calibrate}}}:
\begin{align}
\theta_d &= \theta\,(1 + k_1\theta^2 + k_2\theta^4 + k_3\theta^6 + k_4\theta^8) \\
(x_d,\, y_d) &= \frac{\theta_d}{r}\,(x,\, y)
\end{align}
This gives the distortion coefficient vector $(k_1, k_2, k_3, k_4)$ \cite{OpenCVFisheyeCamera}.

\subsection{In-air calibration, not fitted to water}
This technique uses the standard Brown-Conrady calibration completed in air (using the same rig, same detector), including the stereo extrinsics, applied to the underwater
test data set. Due to the glass water refraction, T1 is expected to fail badly. It is then used as a reference point to test the effectiveness of the Pinax model.

\subsection{Pinax model (in-air calibration with refraction compensation)}
The Pinax model \cite{luczynskiPinaxmodelAccurateEfficient2017} corrects for refraction at a flat port using only an
in-air calibration. Behind a flat port, the rays do not pass through a single point but all cross one line, the axis
through the camera centre normal to the port, so the camera is strictly an axial rather than a pinhole camera
\cite{agrawalTheoryMultilayerFlat2012}. When the camera sits close to the port, the rays cross this axis over a very
short section, and Pinax exploits the fact that the axial camera is then well approximated by a virtual pinhole camera.

In the standard implementation, the camera sits a distance $d_0$ behind a glass port of thickness $d_1$. The centre
of the virtual pinhole camera is placed on the optical axis, at the middle of the section where the rays traced back
from the water cross the axis. Each pixel of the virtual pinhole image is projected to a point on a plane 5\,m
in front of the camera, and that point is projected back through the water, glass and air into the physical camera
with the analytical forward projection of \cite{agrawalTheoryMultilayerFlat2012}, which follows from Snell's law. This
uses the in-air intrinsics and lens distortion, $d_0$, $d_1$ and the refractive indices of the glass and water. The
mapping is computed once and stored as a lookup table, and each underwater image is then corrected by a single
remapping step that removes the lens distortion and the refraction together. The corrected images can then be used
with the standard pinhole pipeline.

In this work, the mapping is applied directly to the detected corners instead of to whole images. Each corner is
undistorted with the in-air Brown-Conrady model to recover its ray in air, and the ray is refracted into water with
Snell's law, $\sin\theta_\text{air} = n_w \sin\theta_\text{water}$ with $n_w = 1.333$. Projection runs the same
steps in reverse. Because the Aquagon is a water-contact lens, $d_0$ and $d_1$ have no physical value. They set where
the virtual centre sits on the optical axis, which the fitted board pose absorbs, and how far the rays spread about
it, which is not modelled here. The glass index cancels out of the ray direction, so only the ray directions are
modelled. No intrinsic parameters are fitted to the underwater data. As for the other models, only the board poses
and the stereo extrinsics are fitted underwater.

\subsection{Brown-Conrady with axial shift (no single-viewpoint assumption)}
% DONE BY 5:30
% Assumptions made by the pinhole (and Brown-Conrady) models regarding incoming light and center - single viewpoint
% Refraction bends the rays
% Different angles, cross at different points , edge futher forward or back
% 
To test whether the Aquagon lenses act as a single-viewpoint camera, the Brown-Conrady model is extended so that
each ray can start from a point further along the optical axis. This is a simplified form of Gennery's model
\cite{genneryGeneralizedCameraCalibration2006}, in which the starting point moves along the axis as rays come in
further from the centre. Here the shift depends on the
normalised radius:
\begin{equation}
\Delta z(r) = a_1 r^2 + a_2 r^4, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}.
\end{equation}
If $a_1=a_2=0$, every ray starts at the origin making it a standard Brown-Conrady. If the $a_1$ and $a_2$ parameters do not
reduce error on held-out images, the extra shift is not helping and the camera behaves as a single-viewpoint camera.

The model is fitted by starting from the standard \texttt{cv2.calibrateCamera} solution ($a_1 = a_2 = 0$)
and refining all parameters, including $a_1$ and $a_2$, with a bundle adjustment that minimises reprojection
error, where each point's ray is found iteratively because its starting point depends on $r$.

\subsection{Frame selection}
\label{subsec:ch5.frame_selection}
The StellarHD cameras cannot capture single still images. Each frame is extracted from the .mkv video file using
\texttt{extract\_stereo\_frames.py}. As there is no hardware synchronisation between the two StellarHD cameras,
 the script pairs left and right frames with the closest timestamp, rejecting pairs with a difference above a configurable 
 threshold (default 10\,ms). Since the \gls{rov} is stationary at each set position, a 10\,ms offset
causes negligible relative motion between the two views. \texttt{extract\_stereo\_frames.py} allows for manual frame selection 
or selecting frames at set steps (e.g. every 10th frame).

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{subsec:ch5.calibration}

Calibration is performed by \texttt{Calibration.py}, which takes the full set of calibration images and a \gls{json} file of OpenCV detector settings as input.
The geometry of the AprilGrid board is hard-coded in the script, and is used to build a board object that matches the physical target.
Tags are detected in a single-channel image (greyscale, luma or one colour channel, as selected, or several channels merged), and their corners are then refined to sub-pixel accuracy on the greyscale image.
Images with fewer than 10 detected tags are rejected, and misplaced tags and badly fitting images are removed by the outlier rejection described in \autoref{subsec:ch5.optimising_detection}.
The remaining detections, together with the known tag positions on the board, are used to estimate the intrinsics of each camera with \href{https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d9/d0c/group__calib3d.html#gae7f5b1b89ac2d6b1c4bd6a8ad0e5e8c1}{\textcolor{blue}{\texttt{cv2.calibrateCameraExtended}}}.
The stereo extrinsics are calculated with \texttt{cv2.stereoCalibrate}, with the intrinsics held fixed.
The resulting parameters are written to a YAML file, along with the error metrics used in \autoref{subsec:ch5.metrics}, so that calibrations can be compared and passed on to the rest of the pipeline.
An example image with annotated detections is shown in \autoref{fig:ch5.detectedtags}.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{subsec:ch5.metrics}
Optimising for calibration needs careful consideration to avoid reducing the accuracy of the calibration. Selectively choosing frames to reduce \gls{rms} error results in losing calibration
coverage. Harder to detect tags may increase \gls{rms} errors due to higher noise with their detections, resulting in worse \gls{rms} error. This may punish algorithms for a more accurate output.
To avoid this, five metrics are used to evaluate the effectiveness of the calibration:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tag detection rate:} number of AprilTags detected, as a fraction of the total board tags.
    \item \textbf{Mean per-image \gls{rms}:} reprojection error computed separately for each image, then averaged over all images.
    \item \textbf{Overall \gls{rms}:} reprojection error over all detected corners, as returned by \texttt{calibrateCameraExtended}.
    \item \textbf{Measurement accuracy:} error in tag size measured by stereo triangulation of a different AprilTag target at distances of 50 to 250\,cm.
    \item \textbf{Matched-tag \gls{rms}:} reprojection error computed only over tags detected in all of the compared calibrations.
\end{enumerate}

The detection rate was noticeably lower in air than in water. This seems
counterintuitive, since water introduces haze and reduces contrast. 
The likely cause is that the Aquagon lenses are designed specifically for underwater use. 
As was shown in \autoref{fig:ch5.air_water_comp}, the in air images produce a wider field of view with distortion.
Another possible reason could be how the camera is handling the \gls{mjpeg} compression.
If the camera caps the size of each compressed frame, the more textured lab background would force heavier compression and blur fine detail on the target.

In water, the four single channels had similar detection rates, with luma the highest (0.354) and blue
the lowest (0.299). The left and right cameras differed in accuracy. Their median \gls{rms} errors were within
7\,\% of each other for every input except blue (17\,\%), but the right camera's \gls{rms} error was about
70\,\% higher than the left's (49\,\% for blue). The likely cause is that in DS1, the board is only placed at
the centre and left positions, which put it further from the right camera and left more of its tags
occluded in the close up frames. This difference does not appear in air, probably because of the
wider field of view.

This trend was consistent across all distances and is plotted in (\hyperref[app:recall_distance]{Appendix~E})

When looking at which tag detections were made for each channel, an interesting observation was made. Although they had a similar detection rate,
not all the detections were of the same tags. \autoref{fig:ch5.channel_overlap} below shows which tags were detected by each channel. 
The \texttt{Calibration.py} script was subsequently adapted to also take a merged configuration, running the tag detection on
the greyscale and \gls{rgb} channels separately and merging the detected tags. This improved the detection rate up to 0.432.

\subsection{Method}
The detector was tuned by hand and with a coordinate-descent sweep (\texttt{sweep\_detector.py}) over
OpenCV's ArUco \texttt{DetectorParameters}, covering thresholding, contour filtering and bit decoding
(\autoref{tab:detector_sweep_params}), on the 230 underwater calibration images.
The two parameters that describe the printed board, \texttt{markerBorderBits} and
\texttt{errorCorrectionRate}, were set by hand and not swept.

Each resulting configuration was evaluated by calibrating with it, using the standard outlier
rejection. A tag was dropped if its worst corner exceeded the larger of 2\,px and five times the
median. An image was dropped if its corner \gls{rms} exceeded the larger of 1\,px and three times the
median, or if it had fewer than 10 tags.


\subsection{Results and analysis}
The sweep converged after three rounds (133 configurations, about 18 minutes). The final detector parameters can be 
found in  \hyperref[app:detector_configs]{Appendix~E}. The improvements over the OpenCV default detector are given in
\autoref{tab:detector_sweep_result} below.

\subsection{Held-out error and split training}
\label{sec:heldout}
Each method was tested on recordings it was not trained on. Two of the eight underwater recordings were held
out, the method was fitted on the remaining six, and it was then tested on the two held out. This was repeated
for every possible pair, giving 28 splits (\hyperref[app:heldout]{Appendix~E}).

To test an image, the fitted intrinsics are kept fixed and only the board pose is fitted. The held-out error is
the \gls{rms} distance between the detected corners and the corners reprojected by the model. The reported result is
the mean over the 28 splits.

\subsection{Reprojection error on the held-out recordings}

The analysis is split into \textbf{zones} based on how far each corner lies from the principal point, to analyse distortion and
refraction, which both grow towards the edges of the image. The zones are defined using a normalised radius 
from the centre of the sensor (method in \hyperref[app:heldout:zones]{Appendix~E}).
$r = 0$ is at the
optical centre and $r = 1$ at the farthest corner. 

%The image is divided into three zones: centre ($r < 0.33$), mid ($0.33 \le r < 0.66$) and outer ($r \ge 0.66$).

The results are further split into \textbf{near} and \textbf{far} bands by board distance. Near covers boards at 200 to 800\,mm and far
covers boards at 1000 to 1500\,mm. For the near and far comparisons, only the centre zone of the image is compared because
further boards appear small and do not reach the outer zones.

\subsection{Stereo scale}
% Explain what is computed and compared
For each technique, the board corners in the held-out stereo pairs are triangulated using that technique's
calibration of both cameras and the stereo extrinsics between them. The central models (T1-T4) use 
\href{https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d9/d0c/group__calib3d.html#gad3fc9a0c82b08df034234979960b778c}{\textcolor{blue}{\texttt{cv2.triangulatePoints}}}
 on the normalised rays; however, T5 cannot use this method as it does not have a single P matrix to describe it. 
T5 uses midpoint triangulation. Due to small errors in corner detection and calibration, the two rays pass close to each other without 
intersecting. The midpoint triangulation method finds the shortest segment connecting the two and takes the midpoint as the three-dimensional point.
As a check, with $a_1$ and $a_2$ set to zero (making T5 central), the midpoint method agreed with \texttt{cv2.triangulatePoints} to within $10^{-6}$\,mm.

Trends in the residuals are the main indicator of a calibration fault \cite[p.~44]{luhmannSensorModellingCamera2016}. 
An error in the focal length scales every point about the principal point and appears as 
a radial pattern, while an error in the principal point shifts every point equally and appears as a uniform one. 

The T1 arrows are short at the centre and grow longer towards the edges, where most point outwards. 
The observed corners therefore lie further from the centre than the in-air model predicts, which is 
the pattern expected from uncorrected refraction. T2 has much shorter arrows and almost none in the centre,
 but large errors remain at the edges. At the bottom and right edges these point inwards, possibly the result of overcorrection.

The errors for T3 to T5 are far smaller, which is why they are drawn at $\times100$. They are negligible 
across the centre and appear only in parts of the outer region, at the edges of the area the board covered. 
The same small errors also appear along the bottom of the area the board covered, which is the lower
edge of the calibration data but not the edge of the image. This suggests that they come from being at
the edge of the calibration data, not from the edge of the lens. This highlights the importance of covering the whole image with calibration data.
The T3 to T5 plots look almost identical, so this remaining error does not depend on the camera model.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{subsec:ch5.calibration_compensation_conclusion}
% Conclude that the standard is the best option, within noise looking acrcoss left and right cameras.
Experiment 3 shows that, for the Aquagon optics, calibrating underwater far outperforms an in-air calibration, 
even when the in-air calibration is corrected for refraction with the Pinax model. 
The underwater Brown-Conrady calibration had a held-out error of 0.29\,px, against 8.6\,px 
for the in-air calibration and 1.9\,px for Pinax. In every test the error accumulated in the 
outer region of the image. Adding an axial shift (T5) gave no meaningful improvement over 
standard Brown-Conrady, so the Aquagon optics do follow the single-viewpoint approximation.

The Brown-Conrady model (OpenCV's \texttt{cv2.calibrateCamera}) was therefore selected 
for this stage of the pipeline. It is the standard model and works directly with OpenCV's
 stereo rectification and triangulation. On the independent target the underwater models
  measured tags to within about 2\,mm (1.5\,\%). 

A handheld calibration set was subsequently recorded underwater using this method, covering the
 full image including the bottom region not reached in the tests. It gave reprojection errors 
 of 0.28 and 0.27\,px for the two cameras, 0.43\,px for the stereo pair, and a baseline of 150.6\,mm. 
 The resulting camera parameters are given in \autoref{tab:main_calibration} in \hyperref[app:main_calibration]{Appendix~E}.
