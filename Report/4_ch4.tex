\section{Hardware}
\label{sec:ch4.hardware}
%=====================================================================
% Order: cameras -> how they are mounted -> lighting -> calibration target.
 
This project uses an existing BlueROV2 Heavy, owned by UCT.
The system is designed by Blue Robotics and is
a low-cost, high-performance underwater ROV for research and inspection
\cite{bluerobBlueROV2}. The setup system has six thrusters. The Heavy configuration adds two more,
giving the ROV active roll and pitch control  \cite{bluerobBlueROV2Heavy}.
 
In addition to the vehicle's standard forward monocular camera,
two Deepwater Exploration (DWE) stellarHD cameras are fitted in a stereo camera configuration, 
with a 150mm base line. Both have Aquagon lenses and are mounted to the lower front of the frame.
Four Lumen subsea lights at the front of the ROV provide controlalble lights
 \cite{bluerobLumen}.

    \centering

    cameras mounted on the BlueROV2, with the baseline marked.}

The vehicle is controlled by a Raspberry Pi with a
Blue Robotics Navigator flight controller. The Navigator is a Pi
expansion board  that provides the IMU, compass,
barometer, ADC and PWM outputs for the thrusters and lights
\cite{bluerobNavigator}. A Doppler velocity
log (DVL), a scanning sonar, and the three cameras are connected over USB and ethernet ports.
% TODO: confirm models (e.g. Ping360 is a *mechanical scanning* sonar, not side-scan).
connect to the Pi over its USB and Ethernet ports.
 
The ROV was already assembled and configured when this research began, but
the stereo camera feeds were dropping out. The cause was traced to USB
bandwidth. All three cameras shared a single USB bus, which was being throttled. The
original Raspberry Pi~4 was replaced with a Raspberry Pi~5. The new Pi~5's
two independent USB controllers allowed the cameras to be split across
separate controllers, eliminating the dropouts.

\label{subsec:ch4.cameras}

The StellarHD cameras have DWE's Aquagon lenses, which are designed to counteract the effects of the underwater environment \citep{dweAquagon}, 
correcting for distortion and chromatic aberration.
Corrective optics address this by placing additional lens elements behind the port, so that the camera sees a virtual 
image equivalent to the scene in air \citep{slaterAfocalViewportOptics2014}.
The Aquagon lenses have seven elements, two of them aspherical and two extra-low dispersion, 
behind a sapphire front port. The manufacturer quotes distortion below 1.5\,\% in water \citep{dweStellarHDDatasheet}.
The key camera specifications are listed in \autoref{table:ch4.cameraspecs}.

\section{Control and processing laptop} 
The BlueROV2 connects to a laptop through its tether and a tether interface
adapter \cite{bluerobFXTI}. The laptop runs Blue Robotics' Cockpit software \cite{bluerobCockpit}, and the ROV is piloted
with an Xbox controller. The same laptop, a Lenovo LOQ 15IRX10 \cite{lenovoLOQ15IRX10} running
Ubuntu 26.04 LTS, was used for all post-processing in this project. It has an
Intel Core i7-14700HX CPU, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 Laptop GPU with 8\,GB of
VRAM and 32\,GB of DDR5 RAM. All computation completed for
in this project was done on this machine.

\subsection{Calibration Board and test Rig Construction}


% Calibration board
As explained in \autoref{subsec:ch3.targets}, an AprilGrid can still be used for
calibration when only some of its tags are detected. To get a target that is flat and
waterproof, a custom AprilGrid was generated with Kalibr
\citep{furgaleUnifiedTemporalSpatial2013, kalibrRepo} and printed on white aluminium
Dibond by Orms Print Room \citep{ormsPrintRoom}. The board was sized as A1 with 50\,mm
tags, to match standard Kalibr targets \citep{calibioKalibrTargets}. Due to a
miscommunication, it was printed as A2 instead, which reduced the tag size to 36.56\,mm.

% Test rig
A custom sliding rig was designed and manufactured 
using 2020 aluminium extrusions.
The ROV is secured to 3D-printed rollers, fitted with ball bearings, that run along the top and bottom faces of the horizontal extrusions.
 The rig allows
one-degree-of-freedom sliding towards 
and away from the calibration board mounted at its end. 
The board is attatched with a variable angle
mount, allowing it to be set at angles rangeing
 from horizontal to vertical in intervals of 10 degrees.
  The board can be mounted
to the center, right or left of the end of the end rail.
 This allows for repeatable position, distance and
  relative angle between the board and the cameras
across different tests.

\subsection{Fake Sea ice}
While the 3D prints are useful for measuring accuracy, they do not
represent sea ice well. The black surfaces differ from ice in both texture and contrast. 
Furthermore, under-ice scanning looks upwards towards the
surface, where incoming sunlight affects the images. To better approximate real ice, four foam structures were
cut with a hot wire and painted light blue. The set includes a grid of
2\,$\times$\,2\,cm cubes to emulate repetitive patterns, an overhang to emulate large
occlusions, a stepped pyramid to emulate layered ice, and a wave pattern to
emulate curved ice features. \autoref{fig:ch5.foam_blocks} shows the four
blocks and \autoref{fig:ch5.foam_test}

\subsection{DS1: Calibration recordings}
\label{sec:ch4.ds1}
The calibration dataset was recorded on the test rail, with the calibration board mounted at one end.
The board was mounted at two positions across the rail, centre and left. Its angle, measured from the
horizontal, was set to 0, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60\,$^\circ$ at the centre and 0, 20, 40 and 60\,$^\circ$ on the left.
At each angle, recordings were taken every 10\,cm at distances from 40 to 150\,cm.
This test was repeated both in air in the ARU experimantal space, and underwater in a students pool.

\subsection{DS2: Side scans of the 3D prints}
To 3D printed targets were fixed to a flat board with AprilTags placed around them. The tags provide a ground truth
for pose (\autoref{sec:XXX}) and serve as reference points for separating the objects.
The ROV was rolled past the board, stopping every 10\,cm. This was repeated with the stereo cameras
20, 30, 40, 50 and 60\,cm from the board.

\subsection{DS3: Upward-looking scans of the foam blocks}
The four blocks (pyramid, overhang, wave and cubes) were held in place in the water while a swimmer
moved the ROV around below them, with the cameras facing directly upwards.
Each block was scanned separately, next to a flat foam block carrying AprilTags. As in DS2, the tags
provide a ground-truth pose reference. Each scan consists of a single pass. Because the ROV was not fixed
in place, the distance to the blocks was found by triangulating the AprilTags.

The scans were repeated in two conditions: in direct sunlight at around 13:00, and in overcast
late-afternoon light at around 17:00. \autoref{fig:ch4.data_up} shows a scan of the pyramid in the
darker conditions.
