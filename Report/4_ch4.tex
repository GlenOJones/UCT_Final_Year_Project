\label{sec:ch4.hardware}

This project uses an existing BlueROV2 Heavy, owned by UCT.
The system is designed by Blue Robotics markets as
a low-cost, high-performance platform for research and inspection
\cite{TODO-bluerobotics-bluerov2}. The Heavy configuration adds two vertical
thrusters to the standard six, giving full six-degree-of-freedom control
 \cite{TODO-bluerobotics-bluerov2}.
 
In addition to the vehicle's standard forward monocular camera
% TODO: name the stock camera model on UCT's unit (check the vehicle).
two Deepwater Exploration (DWE) stellarHD cameras are fitted in a stereo camera configuration, 
with a 150mm base line. Both have Aquagon lenses and are mounted to the lower front of the frame.
Four Lumen subsea lights at the front of the ROV provide controlalble lights
 \cite{TODO-bluerobotics-lumen}.

The vehicle is controlled by a Raspberry Pi with a
Blue Robotics Navigator flight controller. The Navigator is a Pi
expansion board  that provides the IMU, compass,
barometer, ADC and PWM outputs for the thrusters and lights
\cite{TODO-bluerobotics-navigator}. A Doppler velocity
log (DVL), a scanning sonar, and the three cameras are connected over USB and ethernet ports.
% TODO: confirm models (e.g. Ping360 is a *mechanical scanning* sonar, not side-scan).
connect to the Pi over its USB and Ethernet ports.
 
The ROV was already assembled and configured when this research began, but
the stereo camera feeds were dropping out. The cause was traced to USB
bandwidth. All three cameras shared a single USB bus, which was being throttled. The
original Raspberry Pi~4 was replaced with a Raspberry Pi~5. The new Pi~5's
two independent USB controllers allowed the cameras to be split across
separate controllers, eliminating the dropouts.

\subsection{Calibration Board and test Rig Construction}


% Calibration tests (in air vs underwater)
As explained in \autoref{subsec:ch3.targets}, an AprilGrid can still be used for
calibration when only some of its tags are detected. To get a target that is flat and
waterproof, a custom AprilGrid was generated with Kalibr
\citep{furgaleUnifiedTemporalSpatial2013, kalibrRepo} and printed on white aluminium
Dibond by Orms Print Room \citep{ormsPrintRoom}. The board was planned at A1 with 50\,mm
tags, to match standard Kalibr targets \citep{calibioKalibrTargets}. Because of a
miscommunication, it was printed at A2 instead, which reduced the tag size to 36.65\,mm.

A sliding rig was designed using 2020 aluminium extrusions and custom 3D-printed sliding mounts for the ROV. The rig allows
one-degree-of-freedom sliding towards and away from a calibration board mounted at its end. The board is attatched with a variable angle
mount, allowing it to be set at angles rangeing from horizontal to vertical in intervals of 10 degrees. The board's mount can be mounted
to the center, right or left of the end of the end rail. This allows for repeatable position, distance and relative angle between the board and the cameras
across different tests.

Design around a turbidity of 3 NTU, choosing AprilTag for the worst case scenario.

The paper expresses detected marker size in pixels. At an FTU of 3.6, the detected marker size is as low as 21 pixels. In order to evaluate the capability of the marker systems to detect small markers in turbid underwater environments, the camera is centred in front of the markers and then moved further away, increasing the distance between the camera and the markers and therefore decreasing the marker size in pixels.
% TODO: "The paper" - which one? add \cite.

Underwater, the required pixel count rises above the 25-pixel rule: the minimum pixel size required for reliable detection increases significantly. The 25-pixel rule used in air is an absolute lower bound; underwater, aim for 40 to 60+ pixels per tag to maintain sub-pixel corner accuracy. We are going with a minimum of 40 pixels for the maximum turbidity situation.

We want to work with 1-2 metres calibrated, and the minimum focus distance is 25cm.
% NOTE: requirements table says 0.3-2.0 m; keep these consistent.

\textbf{Pin-hole model pixel calculation.} The pixel width $p$ of a tag in the image plane depends on the tag size $S$ (metres), distance $D$ (metres), and focal length $f_x$ (pixels):
\begin{equation}
p = \frac{S \cdot f_x}{D}.
\end{equation}
% Could reference the pinhole model in Ch3 (sec Image Formation) here.
For a standard camera underwater (assuming a $60^\circ$ to $70^\circ$ horizontal field of view in water due to flat-port refraction), the focal length $f_x$ typically ranges between 1200 and 1400 pixels for a 1600-pixel wide sensor. Assuming an average focal length $f_x = 1300$ pixels:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Option A, $65\text{ mm}$ tag size ($S = 0.065\text{ m}$): at 1 m, $p \approx 84.5$ pixels (excellent detection); at 3 m, $p \approx 28.1$ pixels (slightly low for underwater contrast).
	\item Option B, $85\text{ mm}$ tag size ($S = 0.085\text{ m}$): at 1 m, $p \approx 110.5$ pixels (excellent detection); at 3 m, $p \approx 36.8$ pixels (safe margin for underwater blur).
\end{itemize}

Do not use brushed aluminium: the aluminium shows through all white areas of the image, so the white squares are bare metal with low contrast under lights.
