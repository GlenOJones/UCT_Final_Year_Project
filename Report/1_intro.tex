\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}
This report describes the research, design and testing of an underwater stereo camera system for the
three-dimensional reconstruction of the underside of Antarctic sea ice. The system is developed as a proof of concept
on a BlueROV2 tested on targets in a swimming pool.

This chapter outlines the background to the project and why this research is important. It then states the
problem, the project objectives, and the scope, limitations and assumptions, and closes with the plan of development.

%====================================================
\section{Background}
\label{sec:ch1.background}


Although the Southern Ocean 
covers only 20 to 30\,\% of Earth's oceans, 
it absorbs approximately $75\,\%$ of all anthropogenic\footnote{Anthropogenic: caused or produced by human activity, rather than occurring naturally.} and $40\,\%$ of the anthropogenic CO\textsubscript{2} 
 heat taken up by the world's oceans \cite{kennicuttSustainedAntarcticResearch2019}. Each winter, up to 18 million km$^2$ of this ocean
  freezes over \cite{parkinson40yRecordReveals2019}, and this seasonal sea ice plays a central role in both the heat and carbon uptake \cite{rysgaardSeaIceContribution2011}.

The heat absorption is driven by unique wind patterns and deep ocean vertical mixing.
Persistent westerly winds over the Southern Ocean transport surface water north via Ekman drift \cite{Circulation}. This draws deep water masses (Circumpolar Deep Water) 
to the surface, where the cold water pulls heat out of the atmosphere \cite{armourSouthernOceanWarming2016}.
The cold  water moves towards the equator and sinks beneath the warmer subtropical waters, burying the
absorbed atmospheric thermal energy.

There are two main engines driving the absorption of CO\textsubscript{2} in the Southern and Antarctic oceans \cite{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019,kennicuttSustainedAntarcticResearch2019}.
The first is a physical pump. CO\textsubscript{2}'s solubility improves at low temperatures, meaning the cold upwelled water pulls carbon out of the atmosphere \cite{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019}.
The second is a biological pump. The cold upwelled water contains nutrients, inducing large phytoplankton blooms during
the polar spring and summer. The phytoplankton absorb carbon during photosynthesis which then sinks deep into the ocean in the form of dead biomass and faecal matter \cite{kennicuttSustainedAntarcticResearch2019}.

% Ice importatns - NEED MUCH MORE EMPHASIS FOR THE IMPORTANCE OF THE ICE, AND THE UNDERSIDE OF THE ICE.
The ice surrounding the Antarctic continent plays a critical role in these processes as well
as serving key ecological purposes. 
It provides a habitat for sea-ice algae, which act as primary producers in the Antarctic food web during low-productivity periods \cite{lizotteContributionsSeaIce2001}.
%and contributes to the carbon pump that increases net CO\textsubscript{2} uptake through brine rejection and further processes \cite{rysgaardSeaIceContribution2011}.


 The region between the main ice pack and the open ocean, where \gls{sic} ranges from 15 to 80\,\%, is
 typically defined as the \gls{miz} \cite{dumontMarginalIceZone}.
 In the \gls{miz}, ice forms rapidly as pancake ice, which consists of circular disks that are continually subjected to ocean swells. The formation
 of this pancake ice is examined in \autoref{sec:ch2.pancake_ice}.
 This accelerated freezing causes intense brine rejection, where the excluded salt increases surface water density. 
 This high-density water sinks, directly powering the physical carbon pump
 \cite{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019}. 


Much of this exchange occurs at the underside of the ice, forming the interface between the ice and the ocean. 
The shape of the underside determines the drag between the ice and the water and the area over which heat is exchanged. 
It also provides a habitat for sea-ice algae, which act as the main primary producers when the open water is 
least productive \cite{lizotteContributionsSeaIce2001}. Antarctic krill are concentrated in a narrow band 
beneath the ice edge \cite{brierleyAntarcticKrillSea2002}.
Despite this, the underside of pancake ice remains largely unstudied. Satellites infer ice thickness from the 
height of the ice above the waterline, and cannot observe the underside directly \cite{zygmuntowskaUncertaintiesArcticSea2014}. 
While the upper surface of pancake ice has been reconstructed at centimetre resolution \cite{spirakis3DReconstructionPancake2023}, 
no equivalent measurements exist for the underside.
Direct, fine-scale observations of the underside of pancake ice are therefore needed to better understand heat exchange and carbon uptake in the region, and the habitat the ice provides.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:ch1.problemStatement}
%====================================================

No three-dimensional reconstructions of the underside of Antarctic pancake ice have been conducted according to publicly available data.
 Above-ice methods
cannot see the underside, and multibeam sonar is too coarse to resolve its centimetre-scale structure. A calibrated
stereo camera pair could capture this detail attached to an ROV, as shown in \autoref{fig:ch1.rov_ice_setup}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figs/ch1/ROVIceSetup.pdf}
	\caption[BlueROV2 mapping the sea ice underside]{\textbf{BlueROV2 mapping the sea ice underside.} The tethered \acrshort{rov} looks upwards with a stereo camera pair, and points on the ice underside are seen by both cameras.}
	\label{fig:ch1.rov_ice_setup}
\end{figure}

Refraction, scattering,
low texture and uneven lighting may cause each stage of a stereo reconstruction pipeline to fail. 
This project examines
whether the BlueROV2 stereo cameras can reconstruct an overhead underwater surface with measurable accuracy, and which
methods are best suited to each stage of the pipeline.

\section{Project objectives}
\label{sec:ch1.projectobjectives}
%====================================================
% General goal to test underwater environemner, using ROVYn

% NOt a fully fledged ready to deploy, but rather a proof of concept and analyse of how it might work
The aim of this project is to research and carry out an initial proof of concept for three-dimensional stereo underwater reconstruction using the BlueROV2. 
The first objective is to investigate different reconstruction implementations and select the most suitable methods for the platform. 
The second is to design and build a test rig that allows stereo imagery to be collected in a controlled environment. The third is 
to use this rig to generate a dataset of targets with known geometry, allowing the accuracy of the reconstruction to be quantified. 
The final objective is to discuss the performance of the system and recommend improvements towards under-ice deployment

\section{Scope, limitations and assumptions}
\label{sec:ch1.scope}

This project specifically looks at the use of camera optics. Other sensing methods, such as sonar and LiDAR, are outside its scope. Existing open-source methods for
calibration, depth estimation, pose estimation and frame fusion are compared rather than new algorithms developed.
All processing is done offline after recording, and real-time operation is not considered. Testing is limited to the
laboratory and a swimming pool, and no deployment under real sea ice was carried out.

% Limitation
The project was limited to approximately 13 weeks, from 27 July to
27 October. In this
period, the necessary computer vision background and workings of the ROV had to be learnt, dataset generated, and pipeline
coded and analysed. The testing did not include any full under-ice deployments.
The project is limited to the existing BlueROV2 Heavy and its two StellarHD cameras, which are not synchronised in
hardware. Conditions in the pool differ from those beneath sea ice. The water is fresh, without frazil
crystals or other suspended particles, and the tests were carried out in natural daylight rather than the very low
light found beneath pack ice. The mock ice targets also differ from real ice in texture, colour and translucency. 


%Assumptions
It is assumed that the main features of the ice underside, such as overhangs, steps and centimetre-scale roughness,
can be represented by simple foam shapes based on the literature and the photographs in \autoref{sec:ch2.pancake_ice}.
The targets are assumed to be stationary relative to the vehicle during a scan, and the cameras are assumed to remain
rigidly mounted. The 3D-printed targets are assumed to match
their design geometry closely enough to serve as ground truth. %fixes

\section{Plan of development}
\label{sec:ch1.planofdevelopment}
The report begins with a literature review in \autoref{ch:review}, covering the formation of Antarctic pancake ice and
the features of its underside. The review then compares the techniques that have been used to observe and map the ice, from satellites and
aircraft above to acoustic and optical instruments below. Following this, the challenges of underwater optical
reconstruction are examined, and existing applications of underwater stereo reconstruction are summarised.

Subsequently, \autoref{ch:theory} explains the computer vision theory behind each stage of the reconstruction.
It works from a basic pinhole camera model through lens distortion and camera calibration, including calibration
underwater, to stereo geometry and matching, which give depth from a pair of images. It then covers pose estimation
and the fusion of many frames into a single surface. This theory forms the basis for the methods compared in the
later chapters.

\autoref{ch:design} then describes the methodology, including the hardware and software used, the design of the test
rig and targets, and the datasets recorded. 
\autoref{ch:calibration} calibrates the stereo cameras for the underwater environment, since water and the camera
housing change how light reaches the sensor. The AprilGrid detection is first optimised by choosing the image channel
and tuning the detector settings, and five underwater compensation techniques are then compared against an in-air baseline.

Using the calibrated cameras, \autoref{ch:reconstruction} builds and tests the reconstruction pipeline one stage at a
time. Methods for depth estimation, pose estimation and frame fusion are compared on the 3D-printed and mock ice
targets. The complete pipeline is then tested as
a whole, including how it depends on the number of frames.

Finally, \autoref{ch:conclusions} summarises the findings of the project, and \autoref{ch:recommendations} makes
recommendations for further work towards deployment beneath sea ice.
