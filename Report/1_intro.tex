\section{Background}
\label{sec:ch1.background}


Although the Southern Ocean 
covers only 20 to 30\,\% of Earth's oceans, 
it absorbs approximately $75\,\%$ of all anthropogenic\footnote{Anthropogenic: caused or produced by human activity, rather than occurring naturally.} and $40\,\%$ of the anthropogenic CO\textsubscript{2} 
 heat taken up by the world's oceans \cite{kennicuttSustainedAntarcticResearch2019}. Each winter, up to 18 million km$^2$ of this ocean
  freezes over \cite{parkinson40yRecordReveals2019}, and this seasonal sea ice plays a central role in both the heat and carbon uptake \cite{rysgaardSeaIceContribution2011}.

The heat absorption is driven by unique wind patterns and deep ocean vertical mixing.
Persistent westerly winds over the Southern Ocean transport surface water north via Ekman drift \cite{Circulation}. This draws deep water masses (Circumpolar Deep Water) 
to the surface, where the cold water pulls heat out of the atmosphere \cite{armourSouthernOceanWarming2016}.
The cold  water moves towards the equator and sinks beneath the warmer subtropical waters, burying the
absorbed atmospheric thermal energy.

There are two main engines driving the absorption of CO\textsubscript{2} in the Southern and Antarctic oceans \cite{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019,kennicuttSustainedAntarcticResearch2019}.
The first is a physical pump. CO\textsubscript{2}'s solubility improves at low temperatures, meaning the cold upwelled water pulls carbon out of the atmosphere \cite{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019}.
The second is a biological pump. The cold upwelled water contains nutrients, inducing large phytoplankton blooms during
the polar spring and summer. The phytoplankton absorb carbon during photosynthesis which then sinks deep into the ocean in the form of dead biomass and faecal matter \cite{kennicuttSustainedAntarcticResearch2019}.

% Ice importatns - NEED MUCH MORE EMPHASIS FOR THE IMPORTANCE OF THE ICE, AND THE UNDERSIDE OF THE ICE.
The ice surrounding the Antarctic continent plays a critical role in these processes as well
as serving key ecological purposes. 
It provides a habitat for sea-ice algae, which act as primary producers in the Antarctic food web during low-productivity periods \cite{lizotteContributionsSeaIce2001}.
%and contributes to the carbon pump that increases net CO\textsubscript{2} uptake through brine rejection and further processes \cite{rysgaardSeaIceContribution2011}.


 The region between the main ice pack and the open ocean, where \gls{sic} ranges from 15 to 80\,\%, is
 typically defined as the \gls{miz} \cite{dumontMarginalIceZone}.
 In the \gls{miz}, ice forms rapidly as pancake ice, which consists of circular disks that are continually subjected to ocean swells. The formation
 of this pancake ice is examined in \autoref{sec:ch2.pancake_ice}.
 This accelerated freezing causes intense brine rejection, where the excluded salt increases surface water density. 
 This high-density water sinks, directly powering the physical carbon pump
 \cite{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019}. 


Much of this exchange occurs at the underside of the ice, which forms the interface between the ice and the ocean. 
The shape of the underside determines the drag between the ice and the water and the area over which heat is exchanged. 
It also provides a habitat for sea-ice algae, which act as the main primary producers when the open water is 
least productive \cite{lizotteContributionsSeaIce2001}. Antarctic krill are concentrated in a narrow band 
beneath the ice edge \cite{brierleyAntarcticKrillSea2002}.
Despite this, the underside of pancake ice remains largely unstudied. Satellites infer ice thickness from the 
height of the ice above the waterline, and cannot observe the underside directly \cite{zygmuntowskaUncertaintiesArcticSea2014}. 
While the upper surface of pancake ice has been reconstructed at centimetre resolution \cite{spirakis3DReconstructionPancake2023}, 
no equivalent measurements exist for the underside, and models of the ice-ocean interface simplify the pancake geometry. 
Direct, fine-scale observations of the underside of pancake ice are therefore needed to improve 
understanding of heat exchange, carbon uptake and primary production in the region.

\section{Project objectives}
\label{sec:ch1.projectobjectives}
%====================================================
% General goal to test underwater environemner, using ROVYn

% NOt a fully fledged ready to deploy, but rather a proof of concept and analyse of how it might work
The aim of this project is to research and carry out an initial proof of concept for three-dimensional stereo underwater reconstruction using the BlueROV2. 
The first objective is to investigate different reconstruction implementations and select the most suitable one for the platform. 
The second is to design and build a test rig that allows stereo imagery to be collected in a controlled environment. The third is 
to use this rig to generate a dataset of targets with known geometry, which allows the accuracy of the reconstruction to be quantified. 
The final objective is to discuss the performance of the system and recommend improvements towards under-ice deployment
