\chapter{Theoretical development}
\label{ch:theory}

\section{Introduction}
% Pipeline: calibrated camera → stereo depth → pose → fused surface
This chapter presents the theory behind each stage of the reconstruction pipeline. It begins with how a camera forms an
image and how the camera is calibrated, including the effect of a flat port underwater. It then covers stereo geometry
and matching, which give depth from a stereo pair, and finally pose estimation and fusion, which combine the depth from
many frames into a single 3D surface.

\section{Image formation}
This section describes how a camera maps a 3D world point to a 2D pixel, starting from the ideal pinhole model and
then accounting for real lens distortion. The pinhole and projective camera models follow Hartley and
Zisserman~\cite{hartleyMultipleViewGeometry2004} and the Stanford CS231A course notes~\cite{hataCS231ACourseNotes}.
The intrinsic parameters use the notation of Zhang~\cite{zhangFlexibleNewTechnique2000}, and the lens distortion
model is based on Brown~\cite{brownDecenteringDistortionLenses1966,brownCloseRangeCameraCalibration1971} as
implemented in OpenCV~\cite{opencvCameraCalibration3D}.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The pinhole camera}
\label{subsec:ch3.pinhole}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
% Merge the two sections
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
Before looking at reconstruction, it is important to understand how a camera maps a 3D point
in a scene to a pixel. Every on a 3D object emits or reflects light in multiple directions. If a sensor is placed
in front of the object, each point on the sensor receives light from every point on the object, resulting in a blurred
image. This can be fixed by placing a barrier with a tiny hole (aperture) between the object and the sensor.
Each point's rays therefore land on a single point on the sensor, producing a clear image and a mapping from the 3D object 
to the 2D sensor. This is known as the pinhole camera
model \cite{hataCS231ACourseNotes} and is shown in \autoref{fig:ch3.pinhole_model}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figs/Ch3/pinhole_model2.png}
	\caption[Pinhole camera model]{\textbf{Pinhole Model.} Incoming rays with aperture and barrier.}
	\label{fig:ch3.pinhole_model}
\end{figure}


The following terms are used for the pinhole camera throughout this report \cite{hataCS231ACourseNotes,hartleyMultipleViewGeometry2004}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Camera centre} $O$: the pinhole, through which every ray passes. It is also called the optical centre or centre of projection.
	\item \textbf{Image plane} $\Pi'$: the plane of the sensor, on which the inverted image forms.
	\item \textbf{Focal length} $f$: the distance from $O$ to the image plane.
	\item \textbf{Optical axis}: the line through $O$, perpendicular to the image plane.
	\item \textbf{Principal point}: the point where the optical axis intersects the image plane.
	\item \textbf{Virtual image plane} $\Pi'_v$: an imaginary plane a distance $f$ \textit{in front} of $O$. The image on it is identical to the sensor image except that it is not inverted.
	\item \textbf{Camera coordinate frame}: The \textit{camera frame} with origin at $O$ and axes $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_c$, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_c$ and $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_c$, where $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_c$ lies along the optical axis towards the scene.
\end{itemize}


Suppose $\mathbf{M}_c = [X_c, Y_c, Z_c]^T$ is a point in the camera frame, and $\mathbf{m}' = [x', y']^T$ is 
where it appears on the virtual image plane. Since every point on this plane lies at $Z = f$, only its first two coordinates are needed.
The image coordinates $(x', y')$ can be found from the scene coordinates using similar triangles, as shown in the cross-section of 
\autoref{fig:ch3.pinhole_detailed}.
The smaller triangle is shown in orange and the larger triangle in blue. Both are 
right-angled and share the angle at $O$, so they are similar triangles with their corresponding sides having equal ratios:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{y'}{f} = \frac{Y_c}{Z_c}, \qquad \frac{x'}{f} = \frac{X_c}{Z_c}
	\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
	y' = f\,\frac{Y_c}{Z_c}, \qquad x' = f\,\frac{X_c}{Z_c}.
	\label{eq:ch3.persp}
\end{equation}
 
% TODO: relabel the figure to match the text: M_c, m', f, Z_c and O
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figs/Ch3/pinhole_detailed2.png}
	\caption[Similar triangles in the pinhole model]{\textbf{Similar triangles in the pinhole model.} Cross-section through the optical axis.
	 The triangle formed by $O$, the optical axis and the scene point $\mathbf{M}_c$ is similar to the triangle formed by $O$, 
	 the principal point and the image point $\mathbf{m}'$, which gives \autoref{eq:ch3.persp}.}
	\label{fig:ch3.pinhole_detailed}
\end{figure}
 

\autoref{eq:ch3.persp} gives the equations of \textit{perspective projection} \cite{hataCS231ACourseNotes,hartleyMultipleViewGeometry2004}. 
From these equations two key observations can be made. Objects appear smaller with distance, 
since the image coordinates scale with $1/Z_c$. 
Secondly, and more important to this report, depth is lost in the mapping from 3D to 2D.
Every point along the ray through $\mathbf{M}_c$ projects to the same image point. Recovering depth requires a second 
viewpoint (\autoref{sec:ch3.stereo_geometry}). Setting $f = 1$ gives the \textit{normalised image coordinates}
\begin{equation}
	x = \frac{X_c}{Z_c}, \qquad y = \frac{Y_c}{Z_c},
	\label{eq:ch3.normcoord}
\end{equation}
which depend only on the direction of the ray \cite{hartleyMultipleViewGeometry2004}.
 
Modern cameras use a glass lens instead of a pinhole, which gathers more light while still focusing the rays from each point onto one sensor point.
An ideal thin lens follows the same projection as \autoref{eq:ch3.persp}, with $f$ the lens-to-sensor distance \cite{hataCS231ACourseNotes}. 
As the lenses used in this project are fixed-focus (\autoref{sec:ch4.hardware}), this distance is constant and a single calibration holds for all images.

\subsection{Homogeneous coordinates}
\label{subsec:ch3.homogeneous}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
The division by $Z_c$ makes \autoref{eq:ch3.persp} nonlinear, meaning it cannot be written as a matrix. Homogeneous coordinates
solve this by adding an extra coordinate that holds the scale \cite{hartleyMultipleViewGeometry2004}. A 2D point $[x, y]^T$
now becomes $\tilde{\mathbf{m}} = [x, y, 1]^T$, and a 3D point $\tilde{\mathbf{M}} = [X, Y, Z, 1]^T$. Any multiple of a
homogeneous vector represents the same point, so $[2x, 2y, 2]^T$ still represents $[x, y]^T$. The point is recovered by dividing
by the last element. This lets the division be left until the end, so that projection, rotation and translation can all be
written as matrices and chained together. The perspective projection equations then become:
\begin{equation}
	Z_c
	\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
	=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		f & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & f & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & 0 & 1 & 0
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix} X_c \\ Y_c \\ Z_c \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
	\label{eq:ch3.persp_homog}
\end{equation}
The matrix product gives $[fX_c, fY_c, Z_c]^T$, and dividing by its last element returns \autoref{eq:ch3.persp}. The unknown
scale, here $Z_c$, is often written as $\lambda$. 
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Intrinsic parameters}
\label{subsec:ch3.intrinsics}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\autoref{eq:ch3.persp} gives image positions in millimetres, measured from the principal point. 
A digital image instead uses pixels $\mathbf{m} = [u, v]^T$, measured from the top-left corner. 
The intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{K}$ converts the positions from the image plane (mm) to pixel coordinates \cite{zhangFlexibleNewTechnique2000}:
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{\mathbf{m}} = \mathbf{K}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},
	\qquad
	\mathbf{K} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\alpha & \gamma & u_0 \\
		0      & \beta  & v_0 \\
		0      & 0      & 1
	\end{bmatrix},
	\label{eq:ch3.K}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = [x, y, 1]^T$ are the point's normalised coordinates. The intrinsic matrix parameters are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\alpha$ and $\beta$: the horizontal and vertical focal length in pixels, equal to $f$ multiplied by the number of pixels per millimetre.
	\item $(u_0, v_0)$: the principal point in pixels, usually close to the image centre.
	\item $\gamma$: the skew between the sensor axes. This is negligible for modern sensors 
	and set to zero in OpenCV \cite{opencvCameraCalibration3D}.
\end{itemize}

OpenCV writes $\alpha$, $\beta$, $u_0$ and $v_0$ as $f_x$, $f_y$, $c_x$ and $c_y$. 
Since $\mathbf{K}$ is invertible, $\mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{m}}$ converts a pixel back into the direction of its ray. 


%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Extrinsic parameters}
\label{subsec:ch3.extrinsics}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
Real world points are usually given in a \textit{world frame} fixed in the scene.
The \textit{extrinsic parameters} describe the rotation and translation
 from the world frame to the camera frame \cite{hartleyMultipleViewGeometry2004}:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{M}_c = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{M}_w + \mathbf{t}
	\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
	\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_c = \mathbf{T}_{cw}\,\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_w,
	\qquad
	\mathbf{T}_{cw} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\mathbf{R} & \mathbf{t} \\
		\mathbf{0}^T & 1
	\end{bmatrix},
	\label{eq:ch3.extrinsic}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{R}$ is a $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix, and $\mathbf{t}$ is a translation vector.
  $\mathbf{R}$ rotates without stretching or mirroring.
  Note that $\mathbf{t}$ is the position of the world origin seen from the camera, not the position of the camera itself. Regarding the notation used in this report, 
  $\mathbf{T}_{ab}$ maps points from frame $b$ into frame $a$. This means $\mathbf{T}_{cw}$ corresponds to the \texttt{T\_cam\_world} poses determined in \autoref{ch:reconstruction}. 
The intrinsics stay fixed for a camera, while the extrinsics change with every image.


%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The projection chain}
\label{subsec:ch3.projection}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
Combining the extrinsics, perspective projection and intrinsics gives the pinhole camera model \cite{zhangFlexibleNewTechnique2000}:
\begin{equation}
	\lambda\,\tilde{\mathbf{m}} = \mathbf{K}\,[\mathbf{R} \mid \mathbf{t}]\,\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_w = \mathbf{P}\,\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_w,
	\label{eq:ch3.P}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the depth $Z_c$ and $\mathbf{P}$ is a $3 \times 4$ \textit{projection matrix}. 
This assumes an ideal lens. Lens distortion cannot be written as a matrix, so it is applied separately to
 the normalised coordinates \cite{heikkilaFourstepCameraCalibration1997,opencvCameraCalibration3D}.
  The full projection with distortion is shown in \autoref{fig:ch3.projection_chain},
where $\bm{\kappa}$ holds the distortion coefficients.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Lens distortion}
\label{subsec:ch3.distortion}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
Real lenses do not follow the pinhole model exactly. The main difference is \textit{radial distortion}. For real lenses, magnification can vary slightly
 with distance from the image centre,  so points are pushed towards or away from the principal point. This bends straight lines outwards (\textit{barrel}) 
or inwards (\textit{pincushion}), as shown in \autoref{fig:ch3.radial_distortion} \cite{brownCloseRangeCameraCalibration1971}. 
A smaller \textit{tangential} distortion can occur when the lens is not perfectly centred or parallel to the sensor \cite{brownDecenteringDistortionLenses1966}.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figs/Ch3/radial_distortion.pdf}
	\caption[Radial lens distortion]{\textbf{Radial lens distortion.} A square grid (a) undistorted, (b) with barrel distortion and (c) with pincushion distortion, from the first radial term of \autoref{eq:ch5.bc_x}. Dashed lines show the undistorted grid; the cross marks the principal point.}
	\label{fig:ch3.radial_distortion}
\end{figure}
 
Different methods for mapping normalised coordinates to distorted coordinates are described and compared in \autoref{subsec:ch5.theory_calibration_techniques}.

\section{Camera calibration}
\label{sec:ch3.calibration}

Calibration determines the camera's intrinsics $\mathbf{K}$ and distortion $\bm{\kappa}$,
 which are the same for every image, along with the pose $(\mathbf{R}_i, 
 \mathbf{t}_i)$ of each image $i$.
  It uses a target with a known layout, so the 3D position $\mathbf{M}_j$
   of each point on it is known. These points are detected in each image at pixel positions 
 $\mathbf{m}_{ij}$, using the sharp dark-to-light edges at the corners of the black tags.
 With many points across multiple calibration images
 there are far more equations than unknowns. The best fit values are found
by least squares \cite{zhangFlexibleNewTechnique2000}.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Targets and corner detection}
\label{subsec:ch3.targets}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
Standard checkerboard targets require the whole board to be visible, so that each corner 
can be identified by its position in the grid. 
Fiducial markers such as AprilTags instead encode a unique ID in each tag. This means
 every detected corner can be matched to its known position even when the board 
 is partly out of view or occluded \cite{olsonAprilTagRobustFlexible2011}.
  This project uses an AprilGrid, a board of 
  AprilTags \cite{furgaleUnifiedTemporalSpatial2013}, as AprilTags 
  were the most reliably detected of the markers tested in turbid water
 by dos Santos Cesar et al.~\cite{dossantoscesarEvaluationArtificialFiducial2015}. 
 The calibration procedure is given in \autoref{subsec:ch5.calibration} and the detector settings in \autoref{app:detector_configs}.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Zhang's method}
\label{subsec:ch3.zhang}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
Zhang's method \cite{zhangFlexibleNewTechnique2000} has become the standard approach for camera calibration and works in two stages.
Because the calibration board is flat, the mapping from board points to image points in
 each image is a plane-to-plane transformation, a \textit{homography}.
Each homography combines the intrinsics $\mathbf{K}$, which are the same in every image, with the board's pose, which changes between images.
With three or more images of the board at different orientations, $\mathbf{K}$ can be solved in closed form, and the pose of each image then follows.
This first estimate ignores lens distortion and noise.

In the second stage, all parameters, including distortion, are refined together by
 minimising the \textit{reprojection error}. This is the distance between each detected 
 corner and where the camera model predicts it should appear,
\begin{equation}
	E = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} \left\| \mathbf{m}_{ij} - \pi(\mathbf{M}_j;\, \mathbf{K}, \bm{\kappa}, \mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{t}_i) \right\|^2 .
	\label{eq:ch3.reproj}
\end{equation}
where $\pi(\cdot)$ projects a board point through the full camera model of \autoref{fig:ch3.projection_chain}.
In the OpenCV implementation, this is solved iteratively
 with the Levenberg--Marquardt algorithm\footnote{Levenberg--Marquardt algorithm: An iterative method for non-linear least-squares problems.}
 \cite{marquardtAlgorithmLeastSquaresEstimation1963} as the camera model is non-linear. The model's fit is determined
 by the root-mean-square (RMS) reprojection error in pixels.
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stereo calibration}
\label{subsec:ch3.stereo_calib}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
In a stereo pair, both cameras view the same board.
 If $(\mathbf{R}_l, \mathbf{t}_l)$ and $(\mathbf{R}_r, \mathbf{t}_r)$ are 
 the board's poses in the left and right cameras, the transformation 
 from the left camera frame to the right is \cite{opencvCameraCalibration3D}:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{R}_{rl} = \mathbf{R}_r\,\mathbf{R}_l^T, \qquad \mathbf{t}_{rl} = \mathbf{t}_r - \mathbf{R}_{rl}\,\mathbf{t}_l .
	\label{eq:ch3.stereo_extrinsics}
\end{equation}
As the cameras are rigidly mounted, this transformation is the same for every image pair.
It is refined together with both cameras' parameters by minimising the
 reprojection error in both images. 
 The \textit{baseline} is the distance between the camera centres, 
 $B = \|\mathbf{t}_{rl}\|$.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Calibrating underwater}
\label{subsec:ch3.underwater_calib}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
Underwater cameras are sealed inside a waterproof housing and capture the underwater scene through either a dome or a flat window.
With a flat window, or \textit{port}, light passes from water through
 glass into air, bending at each surface. The angle of this refractive bending
 is defined by Snell's law \cite{hechtOptics2017}:
\begin{equation}
	n_w \sin\theta_w = n_g \sin\theta_g = n_a \sin\theta_a ,
	\label{eq:ch3.snell}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the refractive index and $\theta$ the angle to the port'S normal.
 Near the optical axis, $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta$, so a ray arriving at angle $\theta_w$ reaches the camera 
 at $\tan\theta_a \approx n \tan\theta_w$, where $n = n_w / n_a \approx 1.33$. The image is therefore magnified by about 1.33. 
 The effective focal length increases and the field of view narrows \cite{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}. 

 
% DIAGRAM ADAPTED FROM https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/stamp/stamp.jsp?tp=&arnumber=6248073
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\input{figs/Ch3/snell_port.tikz}
	\caption[Refraction at a flat port]{\textbf{Refraction at a flat port.} 
	A ray from the water bends at both surfaces of the port according to \autoref{eq:ch3.snell}, reaching the camera
	 centre $O$ at a larger angle $\theta_a$ than it left the water at $\theta_w$. The water ray meets the port normal a distance $\Delta x$ behind $O$, so the camera is no longer central.}
	\label{fig:ch3.snell_port}
\end{figure}


Refraction also breaks the pinhole assumption. The refracted rays no longer pass through a single point, so the camera is no longer \textit{central} \cite{treibitzFlatRefractiveGeometry2012}. 
A pinhole model fitted underwater is therefore only exact at certain distances, and its errors depend on how far away the scene is \cite{treibitzFlatRefractiveGeometry2012}.

Shortis~\cite{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015} separates underwater calibration methods into three groups:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Absorb refraction into the lens model.} Calibrating in water with a standard model lets the distortion coefficients account for 
	most of the refraction. This is simple but is only accurate over the distances it was calibrated at \cite{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}.
	\item \textbf{Calibrate in air and correct separately.} The camera is calibrated in air, and refraction is corrected using the port geometry and the refractive index of 
	the water, as in the Pinax model \cite{luczynskiPinaxmodelAccurateEfficient2017}.
	\item \textbf{Model refraction explicitly.} The refraction at the port is modelled explicitly (e.g. tracing each ray through the glass)~\cite{agrawalTheoryMultilayerFlat2012}.
\end{enumerate}

\autoref{subsec:ch5.theory_calibration_techniques} describes the compensation methods tested in this project, and \autoref{subsec:ch5.underwater_compensation_analysis} compares them.

\section{Stereo geometry}
\label{sec:ch3.stereo_geometry}

A single image can determine the ray on which a point lies, but not how far along it the point is in space.
 A second camera, at a known distance from the first, sees the same point along a second ray. The point lies 
 where the two rays meet. The distance between the cameras is the baseline (\autoref{subsec:ch3.stereo_calib}). 
 This section describes the geometry that joins the two views.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Epipolar geometry and triangulation}
\label{subsec:ch3.epipolar}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
The \textit{epipolar plane} is made up of the two camera centres and a point in the scene.
 This plane cuts each image along a line, the \textit{epipolar line}.
  A point seen in the left image must therefore appear somewhere along the corresponding epipolar line in the right image.
  This reduces the search for a matching point from the whole image down to a single line \cite{hartleyMultipleViewGeometry2004}. 
  % The actual search is given in addressed in ref section
  
 With matched left and right points, \textit{triangulation} finds the 3D point where the two rays meet.
 The standard linear method combines the projection equations (\autoref{eq:ch3.P}) of both cameras into one 
 system and solves it by least squares \cite{hartleyMultipleViewGeometry2004}. 
 OpenCV's \texttt{triangulatePoints} uses this method \cite{opencvCameraCalibration3D}. Triangulation produces a 3D point in the left camera frame.
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Rectification}
\label{subsec:ch3.rectification}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
The epipolar lines generally run at an angle across the images.
 \textit{Rectification} virtually rotates both cameras so that their image planes are
  parallel to each other and to the baseline.
   The epipolar lines then become horizontal and aligned, so a point on any row of the 
   left image has its match on the same row of the right image \cite{fusielloCompactAlgorithmRectification2000}. 
   Rectification is applied as an image warp, computed once from the calibration, which also removes lens distortion \cite{opencvCameraCalibration3D}.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Depth from disparity}
\label{subsec:ch3.disparity}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
After rectification, the two cameras act like identical pinhole cameras placed side by side with baseline $B$.
 With the left camera at the origin, a point at horizontal position $X$ and depth $Z$ is at $x_l = fX/Z$ 
 in the left image and $x_r = f(X - B)/Z$ in the right (\autoref{eq:ch3.persp}).
 The difference between these two positions is the \textit{disparity} $d$, and 
 gives depth at that point \cite{szeliskiComputerVisionAlgorithmsa}:
\begin{equation}
	d = x_l - x_r = \frac{fB}{Z}
	\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
	Z = \frac{fB}{d},
	\label{eq:ch3.depth}
\end{equation}
with $f$ and $d$ in pixels and $Z$ in the units of $B$. Points close to the camera
 have large disparities and distant points have small ones. 
 Once $Z$ is known, $X$ and $Y$ can be calculated from \autoref{eq:ch3.persp}, so every matched pixel gives a 3D point.

Since disparity can only be measured to the accuracy of the point matching, 
typically a fraction of a pixel, the depth error grows
 with the square of the distance \cite{szeliskiComputerVisionAlgorithmsa}.
 Furthermore, any  error in the calibrated focal length or
 baseline scales every depth by the same proportion.


\section{Stereo matching}
\label{sec:ch3.stereo_matching}
%=====================================================================
Using \autoref{eq:ch3.depth} requires the disparity of each pixel, 
which means finding pixels that show the same point in the left and right image.
 This is the \textit{correspondence problem}. 
Rectification reduces the search to a single row, but matching is still difficult \cite{scharsteinTaxonomyEvaluationDense2002}.
In areas with little texture, many candidates look alike, and repetitive surface patterns produce several possible matches.
Underwater, scattering and attenuation also reduce contrast, making matches less distinct
\cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990,roserSimultaneousUnderwaterVisibility2014}.

% Might remove this if not really relevant
Most stereo matching methods follow the same four steps \cite{scharsteinTaxonomyEvaluationDense2002}:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Matching cost:} for each pixel, compare its patch in the left image with patches at every possible 
	disparity in the right image, and score how different they look. Together, these scores form a \textit{cost volume}.
	\item \textbf{Cost aggregation:} average the scores over neighbouring pixels to reduce any noise effects.
	\item \textbf{Disparity selection:} choose the lowest-cost disparity for each pixel, either directly (local methods) or while also penalising jumps between neighbouring pixels (global and semi-global methods).
	\item \textbf{Refinement:} estimate disparities to a fraction of a pixel, and remove unreliable ones.
\end{enumerate}
The result is a disparity map, which \autoref{eq:ch3.depth} converts into depth, giving a point cloud.

This project compares three types of matchers in \autoref{sec:ch5.depth_test}.

\section{Pose estimation and fusion}
\label{sec:ch3.pose}

\autoref{sec:ch3.stereo_matching} showed that a point cloud can be generated from each
frame. During a video the camera moves, so every frame's
 cloud is in a different reference frame. 
 To combine them, the pose of the camera in each frame must be determined (\autoref{subsec:ch3.extrinsics}).

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Pose estimation}
\label{subsec:ch3.pose}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
A camera pose is the position and orientation of the camera in the world frame, and it changes as the camera moves
through the scene. The poses are needed to combine the 3D points from every frame into a single reconstruction.
Pose can be determined in several ways, three of which are compared in \autoref{sec:ch5.pose_estimation}.


%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Combining frames}
\label{subsec:ch3.fusion}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
Once a frame's pose is known, the frame's depth map can be
 converted into the world frame. 
Each pixel $\tilde{\mathbf{m}}$ with depth $Z$ is first turned back into a 3D point in the camera frame, and then moved into the world frame using the inverse of the camera pose:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{M}_c = Z\,\mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{m}},
	\qquad
	\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_w = \mathbf{T}_{wc}\,\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_c ,
	\quad \mathbf{T}_{wc} = \mathbf{T}_{cw}^{-1} .
	\label{eq:ch3.backproject}
\end{equation}
Successive frames overlap, so the same surface is captured in
multiple frames. Merging these overlapping points averages out noise and
removes points seen in only a few frames \cite{curlessVolumetricMethodBuilding1996}.
The fusion methods used in this report, described in \autoref{subsec:ch5.fusion_tested}, are based on \textit{voxels}:
  small cubes that divide the scene into a 3D grid, each storing
  information about the surface inside it, which is combined across frames.
%--------------------------------------

\section{Pipeline summary: from 2D images to 3D}
\label{sec:ch3.pipeline_summary}
This chapter has explained the foundational theory needed to turn a set of stereo
 image pairs into a 3D surface. 
\autoref{fig:ch3.pipeline} below shows how the different stages come together.
 Calibration 
(\autoref{sec:ch3.calibration}) gives the
intrinsics, distortion coefficients and stereo extrinsics, which are 
used to undistort and rectify each image pair
(\autoref{subsec:ch3.rectification}). Stereo matching 
(\autoref{sec:ch3.stereo_matching}) then finds the disparity of
each pixel, which is converted to depth (\autoref{subsec:ch3.disparity}) 
to give a point cloud in the camera frame.
Finally, each frame's pose (\autoref{subsec:ch3.pose}) is used to move its points 
into a common world frame, where overlapping
frames are joined into a single surface (\autoref{subsec:ch3.fusion}).
