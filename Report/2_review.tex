\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:ch2.introduction}
This chapter builds on \autoref{sec:ch1.background} by reviewing the underside of Antarctic pancake ice, the existing
methods for observing and mapping it, and the challenges that camera-based methods must overcome.
It begins with the formation of pancake ice and the features of its underside. The limitations of observing sea ice
from above are then described, and in situ acoustic and optical under-ice methods are reviewed.
The challenges of imaging underwater and beneath ice are discussed, followed by existing underwater
three-dimensional reconstruction systems and the disparity between Antarctic and Arctic data.
The mathematical background is covered separately in \autoref{ch:theory}.
The chapter closes with a critique of the literature and the gap this project addresses.

\section{Antarctic pancake ice and its formation}
\label{sec:ch2.pancake_ice}
% DIscuss seasonal Ice
 Antarctic sea ice is predominantly seasonal, fluctuating from a summer minimum of approximately 
3 million km$^2$ to a winter maximum of approximately 18 million km$^2$ \cite{parkinson40yRecordReveals2019}. 
Because the ice is relatively young, its formation processes largely determine its structure. In the central Weddell Sea,
 most ice grows through repeated pancake ice formation at the advancing ice edge, 
rather than by slow freezing onto the ice base that is typical of the Arctic \cite{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.
% Discuss briefly pancake ice

Pancake ice forms when new ice grows in the presence of ocean waves.
The turbulence of the Southern Ocean prevents the freezing surface water from forming a continuous sheet,
so the ice instead forms as a suspension of small, loose ice crystals known as frazil \cite{dobleWeddellPancake2003}. These frazil crystals clump into small cakes that collide due to wave action,
forming raised edges \cite{dobleWeddellPancake2003}. Further towards the solid ice edge, weaker waves allow the pancakes to grow to up to 5\,m across before freezing into
consolidated pancakes. Unlike the pack ice, the pancakes move with the waves rather than forming a thick sheet \cite{dobleDynamicalContrastsPancake}.
\autoref{fig:ch2.pancakecycle} shows the stages of pancake ice formation. Frazil crystals form in the surface layer
and gather into pancakes, which then freeze together and raft over one another. Further rafting builds a thicker,
consolidated cover, while new ice forms in the openings between the floes.

\section{Observing sea ice from above}
\label{sec:ch2.observing_sea_ice}
Satellites are commonly used to measure and estimate properties of sea ice such as thickness and surface roughness
\cite{sandvenSeaIceRemote2023}. This is achieved using various technologies and methods such as radars, optical sensors and altimeters \cite{sandvenSeaIceRemote2023}.
However, both the underside of the ice and its morphology are effectively invisible to spaceborne sensors \cite{zygmuntowskaUncertaintiesArcticSea2014}.
This is because satellites only measure the freeboard,\footnote{Freeboard: the height of the 
 sea ice and snow above the local sea level} which is converted to thickness by assuming hydrostatic equilibrium and
estimating the snow depth and ice density \cite{zygmuntowskaUncertaintiesArcticSea2014,markusIceCloudLand2017}.
Brine-wetted snow further complicates radar altimetry, causing significant 
overestimates of thickness \cite{nandanSnowPropertyControls2020}. Snow depth 
and ice density also remain major sources of uncertainty \cite{zygmuntowskaUncertaintiesArcticSea2014}.


Observing the underside therefore requires in situ measurements from below the ice, using instruments
 moored to the seafloor or underwater vehicles. These instruments can largely be divided into acoustic 
 and optical methods.

The first long-term measurements of the ice underside were completed in November
of 1990, with the Alfred Wegener Institute mooring \glspl{uls}
at six locations across the Weddell Sea \cite{behrendtSeaIceDraft2013}. 
Each sonar measured the time it took an acoustic pulse to be reflected off
the ice underside, inferring distance from the instrument and ice draft.
Data was collected over up to a two-year period, showing strong
variation in ice draft. Each sonar was only able to observe a single 
point as the ice drifted overhead, observing a footprint of 6 to 8\,m in 
diameter \cite{behrendtSeaIceDraft2013}.




%2001	Autosub-2 AUV, single-beam sonar, Weddell Sea	First continuous under-ice draft transect (line profile)	Brierley et al. (2002): doi:10.1126/science.1068574
In 2001, a British team deployed the \gls{auv}
Autosub-2 beneath Antarctic sea ice for the first time, studying Antarctic
krill under the ice edge of the Weddell Sea \cite{brierleyAntarcticKrillSea2002}.
The vehicle also carried an upward-looking single-beam sonar, which recorded
continuous measurements of ice draft along its track
\cite{fanUnderwaterIceAdaptive2023}. A single-beam sonar can only produce a
line profile, measuring the draft directly above the \gls{auv}. In March 2003,
Autosub returned to focus on mapping the sea ice north of Thurston Island in
the Amundsen Sea \cite{banksMeasurementSeaiceDraft2006}. Ice draft was
derived from an upward-looking \gls{adcp}
operating at a depth of around 100\,m. The \gls{adcp}'s four beams, each inclined
at 30$^\circ$, sampled four separate points on the ice
underside, allowing four parallel draft profiles to be recorded along each
transect \cite{banksMeasurementSeaiceDraft2006}.

% More scanning done in x,y,z cruises.

Both single-beam sonars and \glspl{adcp} produce profiles along the \gls{auv}'s track, but
cannot capture the full shape of the ice underside. The first swath mapping of
the underside of sea ice was completed in 2004 by Autosub-II off northeast
Greenland, using a multibeam sonar to generate three-dimensional maps of the
ice \cite{wadhamsNewViewUnderside2006}. In the Antarctic, the same approach
was applied using the SeaBED \gls{auv} in 2010 and 2012, which completed lawnmower
patterns 20 to 30\,m beneath ten ice floes in the Weddell, Bellingshausen and
Wilkes Land regions \cite{williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}.
 This produced three-dimensional maps of the ice underside
covering over 500{,}000\,m$^2$. These multibeam sonar scans were able to produce
horizontal resolution of 0.5\,m \cite{williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}.


While the progression from single to multibeam sonar improves the coverage, the resolution remains
too coarse for the centimetre-scale features of the pancake ice. 
Most surveys have focused on the consolidated pack ice, rather than the ice within the \gls{miz}.% sure about the multibeam sonar...

\section{Optical under-ice imaging}
\label{sec:ch2.optical_imaging}
Under-ice observations have a long history and the earliest documented use
of a \gls{rov} beneath Antarctic sea ice dates to the
Winter Weddell Sea Project in 1986 \cite{hempelAntarcticMarineResearch1988}.
During this expedition, an \gls{rov} equipped with a video camera was used to study
Antarctic krill beneath the pack ice
\cite{marschallOverwinteringStrategyAntarctic1988,langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.
While the study's main aim was biological, the footage also revealed the smooth
bottom surfaces of melting floes and, beneath the near-shore landfast ice,
layers of ice platelets up to 5\,m thick extending as far as 35\,km from the
ice-shelf edge \cite{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.

Three-dimensional surface models can be generated at centimetre or finer resolution using photogrammetry, 
which reconstructs three-dimensional shape from overlapping photographs. 
Under-ice cameras on \glspl{rov} have however mostly been used to estimate 
algal biomass \cite{katleinNewRemotelyOperated2017}.

It was not until 2018 that cameras were used for three-dimensional reconstruction of the Antarctic ice underside. 
Cimoli et al.~\cite{cimoliUnderIceHyperspectralRGB2019} mounted a \gls{rgb} camera and a hyperspectral imager 
on a sled winched along the underside of land-fast ice at Cape Evans, Antarctica.

\Gls{sfm} photogrammetry to produce an
elevation map at sub-millimetre resolution. 

The same group later deployed HIcyBot, an \gls{rov} with a hyperspectral imager and 
stereo cameras. Where acoustic positioning failed under the ice, the cameras
 estimated the vehicle's movement to correct the data \cite{cimoliAdvancingMonitoringNearshore2026}.

Cameras have generally been used for documentation rather than mapping. 
During the year-long MOSAiC expedition, an \gls{rov} recorded upward-looking still images every 5\,s 
beneath Arctic sea ice, while the three-dimensional geometry of the underside was mapped with 
multibeam sonar \cite{anhausUndericeEnvironmentObservations2025}. To understand why camera-based under-ice
reconstructions are rare, the
 next section explains the challenges of underwater imaging.

\section{Underwater and under-ice imaging challenges}
\label{sec:ch2.imaging_challenges}
Optical methods offer far finer resolution than sonar
\cite{kocakFocusRecentDevelopments2008,williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015},
but water is a challenging medium for light, and every image
is degraded before it reaches the sensor \cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}.

Underwater, light is absorbed and scattered. Its intensity falls
off exponentially with distance. Red light is lost within a few
metres, while blue-green light travels furthest
\cite{akkaynakWhatSpaceAttenuation2017}.

Backscatter\footnote{Backscatter: Light reflected back toward the camera by
suspended particles}
is uneven across the frame, putting strain on the camera's dynamic
range\footnote{Dynamic range: The difference between the brightest and darkest light
a sensor can record in a single image.}
\cite{treibitzActivePolarizationDescattering2009}. Forward
scatter\footnote{Forward scatter: Light deflected slightly from its path between
the object and the camera, which blurs fine detail.} blurs fine
detail. This is likely
to be a particular problem in the \gls{miz} with frazil crystals
\cite{shenConceptualModelPancakeice2001}
and marine snow.

Lighting is a further challenge. The light reaching the underside
of the ice depends on the overlying snow and ice, and on ice
concentration \cite{horvatEvidencePhytoplanktonBlooms2022}.
Arndt et al.~\cite{arndtInfluenceSnowDepth2017} measured irradiance\footnote{Irradiance: The
radiant power received per unit area, here expressed as a
percentage of the light at the surface.} below Weddell Sea pack
ice at under 0.1\,\%, too dim for imaging, so artificial lighting
is likely needed. This introduces further problems, including backscatter,
brightness falloff, and uneven illumination
\cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}. Pancake ice adds further
difficulty, as gaps between pancakes let bright light through,
creating contrast that may exceed the camera's dynamic range.

Underwater cameras view the scene through a transparent window, or
\textit{port}, in a waterproof housing. The port may be flat or dome-shaped,
and light bends at the water-glass-air interface
\cite{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}.
Treibitz et al.~\cite{treibitzFlatRefractiveGeometry2012} showed that a camera
behind a flat port no longer has a single viewpoint, so the
standard pinhole model produces distance-dependent errors (see
\autoref{subsec:ch3.underwater_calib}).

The ice underside also offers little texture for feature-based
reconstruction. Surfaces are often smooth
\cite{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}, and features such as air
bubbles are small, low in contrast and repetitive.
Zhao et al.~\cite{zhaoModifyingAffordableROV2021} deployed an upward-looking
stereo camera on a BlueROV2, the same platform used in this work,
beneath freshwater ice in Michigan. Despite adequate light,
visual odometry\footnote{Visual odometry: Estimating a vehicle's motion by tracking
how features in consecutive images shift.} failed because features
were ``ubiquitous and similar'' across the images.

Together, these effects make clean, geometrically accurate images
difficult to capture. This places heavy emphasis on calibration and
image correction. Cameras must be optically corrected or well
calibrated \cite{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}, and
lighting and distance need careful attention
\cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}. The subsequent section
reviews existing three-dimensional reconstruction techniques and how they handle
these issues.

\section{Underwater three-dimensional optical reconstruction }

 is limited, stereo reconstruction is widely used underwater, including  ship-hull inspection

Most systems use a downward-looking stereo pair with a 7 to 9\,cm baseline at an altitude of about 2\,m, 
with strobes to reduce backscatter 
\cite{johnson-robersonGenerationVisualizationLargescale2010,johnson-robersonHighResolutionUnderwaterRobotic2017}. 

Processing is typically done offline afterwards, combining stereo matching, \gls{slam}\footnote{Building a map of the surroundings while estimating the vehicle's position within that map.} or bundle adjustment to correct 
drift, and mesh fusion. Processing times range from roughly the duration of the mission 
\cite{johnson-robersonGenerationVisualizationLargescale2010} to 13.6\,h for 360{,}000 images 
\cite{johnson-robersonHighResolutionUnderwaterRobotic2017}. Only 
Negahdaripour and Firoozfam~\cite{negahdaripourROVStereovisionSystem2006} operated in real time, using shadows cast by surface waves as 
texture on an otherwise featureless surface.

\section{Critique of existing literature}
\label{sec:ch2.critique}
The underside of Antarctic sea ice remains largely unstudied at fine
 resolution. At the time of this project, no three-dimensional
  reconstructions of the underside of pancake ice have been published. 
  This is a significant gap, as the underside forms the interface between the ice and the ocean.
  Its shape determines the drag between ice and water and 
   the area over which heat is exchanged, and it is home to sea-ice algae, 
   which are the main primary producers when the open water is least productive \cite{lizotteContributionsSeaIce2001}. 
   In the \gls{miz}, the rapid freezing of pancake ice drives brine rejection, which feeds the physical carbon pump \cite{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019,rysgaardSeaIceContribution2011}. 
   Direct measurements of the underside are needed to improve models of these processes.

Multibeam sonar has provided the most detailed underside 
maps in the Antarctic to date \cite{williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}, 
but at about 0.5\,m horizontal resolution it cannot
 resolve the centimetre-scale structure of pancake ice that
  optical methods could capture.

The literature also highlights the importance of 
careful calibration and of compensating for the underwater 
environment. Backscatter and forward scatter reduce contrast 
and blur fine detail, and both vary with distance and lighting \cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990,treibitzActivePolarizationDescattering2009}. 
Attenuation and refraction further distort the images, so they must be
 corrected before a reconstruction can be trusted 
 \cite{akkaynakWhatSpaceAttenuation2017,shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}.
 The methodology of this project is designed with these considerations in mind.
