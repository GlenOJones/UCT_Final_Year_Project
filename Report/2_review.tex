\section{Importance of the MIZ and Southern Ocean}
\label{sec:ch2.region}



% Intro sentence as to what the MIZ is, basic importance
Despite making up only $20-30\%$ of Earth's oceans, the Southern Ocean absorbs approximately $75\%$ of all anthropogenic heat and 
$40\%$ of all anthropogenic CO\textsubscript{2} taken up by the world's oceans \citep{kennicuttSustainedAntarcticResearch2019}. 

% Wind taking up heat
This heat absorption is driven by unique wind patterns and deep ocean vertical mixing.
Persistent westerly winds over the Southern Ocean transport surface water north via Ekman drift \citep{Circulation}. This draws deep water masses (Circumpolar Deep Water) to the surface.
The upwelled water is substantially colder than the overlying air, pulling heat out of the atmosphere \citep{armourSouthernOceanWarming2016}.
The cold  water moves towards the equator and continues to absorb atmospheric heat. It then sinks beneath the warmer subtropical waters, burying the
absorbed atmospheric thermal energy.


% C02 Absorbtion - mechanical pump
There are two main engines driving the absorption of CO\textsubscript{2} in the Southern and Antarctic oceans. The first is a physical pump. CO\textsubscript{2}'s solubility improves at low temperatures, 
meaning it is absorbed more effectively into the cold waters of the Antarctic. The upwelled cold water absorbs CO\textsubscript{2}, and its density increases through atmospheric cooling and 
seasonal ice formation. This carbon-rich water then sinks to form Antarctic Bottom Water, taking its absorbed carbon down with it \citep{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019}.

% Biological process
There is also a biological carbon pump that pulls CO\textsubscript{2} out of the atmosphere. The cold upwelled water contains nutrients, inducing massive phytoplankton blooms during
the polar spring and summer. The phytoplankton absorb CO\textsubscript{2} during photosynthesis. This absorbed carbon then sinks deep into the ocean in the form of dead biomass and faecal matter, known
as marine snow. This process effectively transfers atmospheric CO\textsubscript{2} to carbon sediments deep in the ocean \citep{kennicuttSustainedAntarcticResearch2019}.


% Ice importatns
The ice surrounding the Antarctic continent plays a critical role in these processes aswell
as key surving key ecological purposes. 
It provides a habitat for sea-ice algae, which act as primary producers during low-productivity periods \citep{lizotteContributionsSeaIce2001},
and contributes to the carbon pump that increases
 net CO\textsubscript{2} uptake through brine rejection and further processes \citep{rysgaardSeaIceContribution2011}.


The Marginal Ice Zone (MIZ) is typically defined as the region with a sea ice concentration (SIC) of $15-80\%$ , 
spanning the region between the main ice pack and the open sea,
 and \citep{dumontMarginalIceZone}. 
 In the MIZ, ice forms rapidly as pancake ice—circular disks that are continually buffered by ocean swells. 
 This accelerated freezing causes intense brine rejection, where the excluded salt increases surface water density. 
 This high-density water sinks, directly powering the physical carbon pump.
 \citep{gruberOceanicSinkAnthropogenic2019}. 


% What it is, maybe a picture


The sea ice of the Southern Ocean, especially the MIZ, plays a central role in regulating the global climate 
system through its influence on heat absorption, carbon uptake, and primary production. 
Much of this exchange occurs beneath the ice, making the ice-ocean interface critical to understanding these processes.

\section{Antarctic pancake ice and their formation}
\label{sec:ch2.pancake_ice}
% DIscuss seasonal Ice
 Antarctic sea ice is predominantly seasonal, fluctuating from a summer minimum of approximately 
3 million km$^2$ to a winter maximum of about 18 million km$^2$ \citep{parkinson40yearRecord2019}. 
Because the ice is relatively young, its formation processes largely determine its structure. In the central Weddell Sea,
 most ice grows through repeated pancake ice formation at the advancing ice edge, 
rather than by the slow freezing onto the ice base that is typical of the Arctic \citep{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.
% Discuss briefly pancake ice

Pancake ice forms where new ice grows in the presence of ocean waves. Frazil crystals clumps into small cakes that collide due to wave action,
forming raised edges \citep{dobleWeddellPancake2003}. Further towards the solid ice edge, weaker waves allow the pancakes to grow to up to 5\,m across before freezing into
consolidated pancakes. Unlike the pack ice, the pancakes move with the waves rather than forming a thick sheet \citep{dobleDynamicalContrastsPancake}.


% Discuss the underside of Ice in the region and intersting parts
This paper focuses on the underside of pancake ice, which forms the ice--ocean interface that controls both drag and heat exchange. While \citet{spirakis3DReconstructionPancake2023}
reconstructed the upper surface of the ice at centimetre resolution, the underside remains largely understudied, with models simplifying pancake geometry.

\section{Observing sea ice: from above vs from below}
\label{sec:ch2.observing_sea_ice}
Satellite remote sensing provides most of the existing data as it offers near continous coverage over a 
large area throughout the year.
It is however largely limited to the upper surface. Ice thickness can be inferred from freeboard, the height of the ice and snow
above the waterline, measured by laser and radar altimetry
\citep{kacimiAntarcticSeaIce2020}. The accuracy however, is lmited by 
uncertain snow depth. 
The bottom surface and morphology of the underside of the sea
ice is effectively invisible looking from the satellite data.

Observing the underside therefore requires in-situ mesurements from below the ice, using either instruments moored to the sea floor or underwater vehicles.
The measurement instruments used can alrgely be broken into acoustic and optical methods.

Long term measurements of the ice underside were completed in November
of 1990, with the Alfred Wegener Institute mooring upwards-looking sonars (ULS)
at six locations across the Weddell Sea \citep{behrendtSeaIceDraft2013}. 
Each sonar measured the time it took an acoustic pulse to be reflected off
the ice underside, inferring distance from the instrument and ice draft.
Data was collected over up to a two year period, showing strong
variation in ice draft. Each sonar was only able to observe a single 
point as the ice drifted overhead, observing a footprint of 6--8\,m in 
diameter \citep{behrendtSeaIceDraft2013}.




%2001	Autosub-2 AUV, single-beam sonar, Weddell Sea	First continuous under-ice draft transect (line profile)	Brierley et al. (2002): doi:10.1126/science.1068574
In 2001, a British team deployed the autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV)
Autosub-2 beneath Antarctic sea ice for the first time, studying Antarctic
krill under the ice edge of the Weddell Sea \citep{brierleyAntarcticKrillSea2002}.
The vehicle also carried an upward-looking single-beam sonar, which recorded
continuous measurements of ice draft along its track
\citep{fanUnderwaterIceAdaptive2023}. A single-beam sonar can only produce a
line profile, measuring the draft directly above the AUV. In March 2003,
Autosub returned to focus on mapping the sea ice north of Thurston Island in
the Amundsen Sea \citep{banksMeasurementSeaiceDraft2006}. Ice draft was
derived from an upward-looking acoustic Doppler current profiler (ADCP)
operating at a depth of around 100\,m. The ADCP's four beams, each inclined
at 30$^\circ$, sampled four separate points on the ice
underside, allowing four parallel draft profiles to be recorded along each
transect \citep{banksMeasurementSeaiceDraft2006}.

Both single-beam sonars and ADCPs produce profiles along the AUV's track, but
cannot capture the full shape of the ice underside. The first swath mapping of
the underside of sea ice was completed in 2004 by Autosub-II off northeast
Greenland, using a multibeam sonar to generate three-dimensional maps of the
ice \citep{wadhamsNewViewUnderside2006}. In the Antarctic, the same approach
was applied using the SeaBED AUV in 2010 and 2012, which completed lawnmower
patterns 20--30\,m beneath ten ice floes in the Weddell, Bellingshausen and
Wilkes Land regions\citep{williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}.
 This produced three-dimensional maps of the ice underside
covering over 500{,}000\,m$^2$. These multibeam sonar scans were able to produce
horizontal resolution of 0.5\,m \citep{williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}.


While the progression from single to multi beam sonar improves the coverage, the resolution remains
too course for the centimeter scale features of the pancake ice.

\section{Optical under-ice imaging}
\label{sec:ch2.optical_imaging}
Under-ice observations have a long history and the earliest documented use
of a remotely operated vehicle (ROV) beneath Antarctic sea ice dates to the
Winter Weddell Sea Project in 1986 \citep{hempelAntarcticMarineResearch1988}.
During this expedition, an ROV equipped with a video camera was used to study
Antarctic krill beneath the pack ice
\citep{marschallOverwinteringStrategyAntarctic1988,langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.
While the study's main aim was biological, the footage also revealed the smooth
bottom surfaces of melting floes and, beneath the near-shore land-fast ice,
layers of ice platelets up to 5\,m thick extending as far as 35\,km from the
ice-shelf edge \citep{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.

Optical methods use cameras to reconstruct surfaces through photogrammetry, 
providing centimeter-scale or finer resolution. 
However, under-ice cameras on ROVs have mostly been used to estimate 
algal biomass \citep{katleinNewRemotelyOperated2017}, 
and are affected by the imaging challenges described in \autoref{sec:ch2.imaging_challenges}.

It was not until 2018 that cameras were used for 3D reconstruction of the ice underside. 
\citet{cimoliUnderIceHyperspectralRGB2019} mounted an RGB camera and a hyperspectral imager 
on a sled winched along the underside of land-fast ice at Cape Evans, Antarctica. 
Structure-from-motion photogrammetry of a 20\,m transect, imaged from about 1.2\,m, produced 
a digital elevation model of the ice bottom at sub-millimetre resolution.
The same group later deployed HIcyBot, an ROV whose stereo camera 
pair produced 3D reconstructions of the ice underside and helped compensate for vehicle motion 
when acoustic positioning degraded beneath the ice \citep{cimoliAdvancingMonitoringNearshore2026}. 

Even on major campaigns, cameras have generally been used for documentation rather than mapping. 
During the year-long MOSAiC expedition, an ROV recorded upward-looking still images every 5\,s 
beneath Arctic sea ice, while the three-dimensional geometry of the underside was mapped with 
multibeam sonar \citep{anhausUndericeEnvironmentObservations2025}. Optical reconstruction of the 
ice underside therefore remains at an early, proof-of-concept stage, and has been limited to 
land-fast ice rather than the pancake ice of the MIZ.

\section{Underwater imaging challenges: attenuation, scattering, illumination, refraction}
\label{sec:ch2.imaging_challenges}
Optical methods offer far finer resolution than what sonar can achieve \citep{kocakFocusRecentDevelopments2008,williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}. Water however is a difficult medium
for light, meaning every image of the ice is degraded before it reaches the sensor \citep{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}. 

\textbf{Attenuation.} Light passing through water is absorbed and scattered, reducing its intensity 
exponentially with distance. The rate of absorbtion is dependent on wavelength, with red light being lost
within a few meters and blue-green light travelling the furthest. Colour and contrast fall off with range \citep{akkaynakWhatSpaceAttenuation2017}. 

\textbf{Scattering.} Backscatter is,  light reflected into the lens by particles in the water, adds a haze and reduces contrast. It can be uneven 
across the frame, putting strain on the cameras dynamic range \citep{treibitzActivePolarizationDescattering2009}. 
Forward scatter, light from the object slightly deflected on its way to the camera, blurs the 
fine detail. \citet{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990} found that seperating the camera from the light source reduces the volume of
illluminated wate in the field of view, thus reducing backscatter. The scattering is likely to be a particular
problenm in the MIZ, with Frazil crystals \citep{shenConceptualModelPancakeice2001} and the marine snow described in \autoref{sec:ch2.region}.

\textbf{Illumination.} The natural light reaching the underside of the ice depends on the ice and snow 
aswell as the concentratino of the ice \citep{horvatEvidencePhytoplanktonBlooms2022}. \citet{arndtInfluenceSnowDepth2017} measured spectra irradiance (brief expl) under
pack ice in Weddel Sea to be below 0.1\%. This is too dim, and therefore requires artificial lighting.
Artificial lighting produces its own set of problems, with backscatter, rapid brightness falloff with distance
and uneven lighting across the frame \citep{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}. Pancake ice adds further difficulty. Gaps between the pancakes
leght bright light through, creating a strong contrast against the dark ice, producing a constrast
that may be beyond a camera's dynamic range. 

\textbf{Refraction.} Underwater cameras sit within a housing and view the scene through a flat or dome port.
Light bends at the water-glass-air interface \citep{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}. \citet{treibitzFlatRefractiveGeometry2012} proved that a 
camera behind a flat port no longer has a single viewpoint, meaning the standard pinhole model produces 
distant dependendent geometric errors (for further info see section X).

\textbf{Low Texture Environment.}
The ice underside offers little texture for feature-based reconstruction: surfaces are often 
smooth \citep{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}, and visible features such as air bubbles are small, 
low in contrast and repetitive. \citet{zhaoModifyingAffordableROV2021} deployed an 
upward-looking stereo camera on a BlueROV2, the same platform used in this work, beneath 
freshwater sheets ice in Michigan. Despite sufficient natural light, visual odometry failed because 
features were ``ubiquitous and similar'' across the low-contrast images.

These effects combine to make clean, geometrically accurate images
difficult to capture. This places heavy emphasis on calibration and image
correction. Cameras must either be optically correctted or well calibrated \citep{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}, and careful attention
must be paid to lighting conditions and distance from the ice \citep{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}.
The following section reviews existing 3D reconstruction
techniques, and how they approach these issues.

\section{Underwater camera calibration and refraction-compensating optics}
\label{sec:ch2.calibration}
Underwater calibration must account for refraction at the air, housing and water interfaces, which 
increased the principal distance of a camera behind a flat port by a factor of 1.34 
\citep{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}. The most common approach calibrates in water and 
absorbs refraction into the lens distortion parameters, but introduces scale errors outside the 
calibrated range. Explicit ray-tracing models are exact but often housing-specific 
\citep{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}. The Pinax model offers a compromise for flat ports, 
converting images into an equivalent pinhole view so the camera need only be calibrated once in air 
\citep{luczynskiPinaxmodelAccurateEfficient2017}.

Refraction can also be reduced optically. A dome port eliminates refraction if the lens is centred 
exactly at the dome's centre of curvature \citep{kunzHemisphericalRefractionCamera2008,mennaGeometricOpticCharacterization2016}, 
while corrector lenses counteract the aberrations of a flat window \citep{CorrectingLensesUnderwater1960}. 
This project uses two global-shutter stellarHD cameras with Aquagon lenses, a seven-element design 
specified to have less than 1.5\% distortion and a $78\times67^\circ$ field of view in water 
\citep{dweStellarHDTechnicalSpecifications}. However, such optics only approximate a perfect central 
projection and are sufficient alone only for low-accuracy 2D work, so calibration remains necessary 
for 3D reconstruction \citep{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}. In either case, cameras must 
be rigidly fixed with focus locked, under which accuracies of around 0.5\% of measured lengths are 
achievable \citep{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}.
