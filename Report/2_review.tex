\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:ch2.introduction}
This chapter builds on \autoref{sec:ch1.background} by reviewing the underside of Antarctic pancake ice, the existing
methods for observing and mapping it, and the challenges that camera-based methods must overcome.
It begins with the formation of pancake ice and the features of its underside. The limitations of observing sea ice
from above are then described, and in situ acoustic and optical under-ice methods are reviewed.
The challenges of imaging underwater and beneath ice are discussed, followed by existing underwater
three-dimensional reconstruction systems and the disparity between Antarctic and Arctic data.
The mathematical background is covered separately in \autoref{ch:theory}.
The chapter closes with a critique of the literature and the gap this project addresses.

\section{Antarctic pancake ice and its formation}
\label{sec:ch2.pancake_ice}
% DIscuss seasonal Ice
 Antarctic sea ice is predominantly seasonal, fluctuating from a summer minimum of approximately 
3 million km$^2$ to a winter maximum of approximately 18 million km$^2$ \cite{parkinson40yRecordReveals2019}. 
Because the ice is relatively young, its formation processes largely determine its structure. In the central Weddell Sea,
 most ice grows through repeated pancake ice formation at the advancing ice edge, 
rather than by slow freezing onto the ice base that is typical of the Arctic \cite{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.
% Discuss briefly pancake ice

Pancake ice forms when new ice grows in the presence of ocean waves.
The turbulence of the Southern Ocean prevents the freezing surface water from forming a continuous sheet,
so the ice instead forms as a suspension of small, loose ice crystals known as frazil \cite{dobleWeddellPancake2003}. These frazil crystals clump into small cakes that collide due to wave action,
forming raised edges \cite{dobleWeddellPancake2003}. Further towards the solid ice edge, weaker waves allow the pancakes to grow to up to 5\,m across before freezing into
consolidated pancakes. Unlike the pack ice, the pancakes move with the waves rather than forming a thick sheet \cite{dobleDynamicalContrastsPancake}.
\autoref{fig:ch2.pancakecycle} shows the stages of pancake ice formation. Frazil crystals form in the surface layer
and gather into pancakes, which then freeze together and raft over one another. Further rafting builds a thicker,
consolidated cover, while new ice forms in the openings between the floes.

\section{Observing sea ice}
\label{sec:ch2.observing_sea_ice}
Sea ice can be observed either from above, using satellites, aircraft and ships, or from below, using instruments
moored to the seafloor or carried by underwater vehicles. This section reviews above-ice observation methods and why they cannot
resolve the underside of the ice, motivating the under-ice methods reviewed in the following sections.

\subsection{From Above}

Satellites are commonly used to measure and estimate properties of sea ice such as thickness and surface roughness
\cite{sandvenSeaIceRemote2023}. This is achieved using technologies such as radars, optical sensors and altimeters \cite{sandvenSeaIceRemote2023}.
Both the underside of the ice and its morphology remain effectively invisible to spaceborne sensors \cite{zygmuntowskaUncertaintiesArcticSea2014}.
This is because satellite altimeters only measure the freeboard, the height of the ice surface above the waterline,
which is converted to thickness by assuming hydrostatic equilibrium and estimating the snow depth and the densities of the ice and snow
\cite{zygmuntowskaUncertaintiesArcticSea2014,markusIceCloudLand2017}.
Brine-wetted snow further complicates radar altimetry, causing significant 
overestimates of thickness \cite{nandanSnowPropertyControls2020}. Snow depth 
and ice density remain major sources of uncertainty \cite{zygmuntowskaUncertaintiesArcticSea2014}.


Aircraft take a similar approach to satellites, measuring the freeboard with laser altimeters and the snow depth with radar \cite{kwokThreeYearsSea2018}. Helicopter-towed electromagnetic sensors can measure the
distance to the conductive sea water beneath the ice, which, combined with a laser altimeter, gives the total thickness of the
ice and snow \cite{haasHelicopterborneMeasurementsSea2009}. From ships, the thickness can be
estimated by eye, or measured from stereo images, as floes are broken and turned sideways along the hull \cite{worbyThicknessDistributionAntarctic2008},
 \cite{niiokaSeaIceThickness2010Link}.

Measurements from above focus on ice thickness. Even the electromagnetic methods that reach the underside average
over its three-dimensional features \cite{haasHelicopterborneMeasurementsSea2009}, so the shape of the underside is not resolved.
Observing this bottom surface requires in situ measurements from below the ice, using instruments moored
to the seafloor or underwater vehicles. These under ice instruments can largely be divided into acoustic and optical
methods.

The first long-term measurements of the ice underside were completed in November
of 1990, when the Alfred Wegener Institute moored\glspl{uls}
at six locations across the Weddell Sea \cite{behrendtSeaIceDraft2013}. 
Each sonar measured the time it took an acoustic pulse to be reflected off
the ice underside, inferring distance from the sonar and ice draft.
Data was collected over a two-year period, showing strong
variation in ice draft. Each sonar was only able to observe a single 
point as the ice drifted overhead, observing a footprint of 6 to 8\,m in 
diameter \cite{behrendtSeaIceDraft2013}.




%2001	Autosub-2 AUV, single-beam sonar, Weddell Sea	First continuous under-ice draft transect (line profile)	Brierley et al. (2002): doi:10.1126/science.1068574
In 2001, a British team deployed the \gls{auv},
Autosub-II, for the first time beneath the ice edge of the Weddell Sea \cite{brierleyAntarcticKrillSea2002}.
The vehicle  carried an upward-looking single-beam sonar, which recorded
continuous measurements of ice draft along its track
\cite{fanUnderwaterIceAdaptive2023}. A single-beam sonar can only produce a
line profile, measuring the draft directly above the \gls{auv}. In March 2003,
Autosub-II returned to focus on mapping the sea ice north of Thurston Island in
the Amundsen Sea \cite{banksMeasurementSeaiceDraft2006}. Ice draft was
derived from an upward-looking \gls{adcp}
operating at a depth of around 100\,m. The \gls{adcp}'s four beams, each inclined
at 30$^\circ$, sampled four separate points on the ice
underside, allowing four parallel draft profiles to be recorded along each
track \cite{banksMeasurementSeaiceDraft2006}.

% More scanning done in x,y,z cruises.

Both single-beam sonars and \glspl{adcp} produce profiles along the \gls{auv}'s track, but
cannot capture the full shape of the ice underside. The first swath mapping\footnote{Swath mapping: measuring a wide strip either side of the vehicle's track in a single pass, using a fan of sonar beams, rather than a single point directly above the vehicle.} of
the underside of sea ice was completed in 2004 by Autosub-II off northeast
Greenland, using a multibeam sonar to generate three-dimensional maps of the
ice \cite{wadhamsNewViewUnderside2006}. In the Antarctic, the same approach
was applied using the SeaBED \gls{auv} in 2010 and 2012, which completed lawnmower
patterns 20 to 30\,m beneath ten ice floes in the Weddell, Bellingshausen and
Wilkes Land regions \cite{williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}.
 This produced three-dimensional maps of the ice underside
covering over 500{,}000\,m$^2$, achieving a
horizontal resolution of 0.5\,m \cite{williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}.


While the progression from single to multibeam sonar improves the coverage, the resolution remains
too coarse for the centimetre-scale features of pancake ice illustrated in \autoref{fig:ch2.pancake_photos}.
Most surveys have focused on the consolidated pack ice, rather than the ice within the \gls{miz} \cite{sandvenSeaIceRemote2023}.% sure about the multibeam sonar...

\section{Optical under-ice imaging}
\label{sec:ch2.optical_imaging}
Optical under-ice observations have a long history, with the earliest documented use of a \gls{rov} beneath Antarctic sea ice
taking place in 1986 \cite{hempelAntarcticMarineResearch1988}. The Winter Weddell Sea Project, aiming to study krill, equipped
the \gls{rov} with a video camera
\cite{marschallOverwinteringStrategyAntarctic1988,langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.
While the study's main aim was biological, the footage also revealed the smooth
bottom surfaces of melting floes and, beneath the near-shore landfast ice,
layers of ice platelets up to 5\,m thick extending as far as 35\,km from the
ice-shelf edge \cite{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}.

Three-dimensional surface models can be generated at centimetre or finer resolution using photogrammetry, 
reconstructing three-dimensional shape from overlapping photographs. 
Most under-ice cameras on \glspl{rov} have instead been used to estimate 
algal biomass \cite{katleinNewRemotelyOperated2017}. It was not until 2018 that cameras were used for three-dimensional
 reconstruction of the Antarctic ice underside. Cimoli et al.~\cite{cimoliUnderIceHyperspectralRGB2019} mounted 
 an RGB camera and a hyperspectral imager\footnote{Hyperspectral imager: a camera that records each pixel in many narrow, adjacent wavelength bands, rather than only the red, green and blue of a standard camera.} to a sled and winched it along the underside of land-fast ice at Cape Evans, Antarctica.
\Gls{sfm} photogrammetry was used to produce an
elevation map at sub-millimetre resolution. The same group later deployed HIcyBot, an \gls{rov} with 
a hyperspectral imager and 
stereo cameras.
Cameras were used to estimated the vehicle's movement where the acoustic positioning failed under the ice \cite{cimoliAdvancingMonitoringNearshore2026}.

Currently, under-ice cameras are mostly used for piloting and for visual observation of the ice and the organisms beneath it,
while the shape of the underside is measured with multibeam sonar \cite{katleinNewRemotelyOperated2017}.
During the year-long MOSAiC expedition, an \gls{rov} recorded upward-looking still images every 5\,s 
beneath Arctic sea ice, while the three-dimensional geometry of the underside was mapped with 
multibeam sonar \cite{anhausUndericeEnvironmentObservations2025}. To understand why camera-based under-ice
reconstructions are rare, the
 next section explains the challenges of underwater imaging.

\section{Underwater and under-ice imaging challenges}
\label{sec:ch2.imaging_challenges}
Typically, optical methods offer finer resolution than sonar
\cite{kocakFocusRecentDevelopments2008,williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}.
Underwater, by contrast, light is absorbed and scattered, resulting in an exponential decrease in intensity over
distance. These characteristics of light propagation in water decrease the quality of
images compared to above-water applications \cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}.


Additionally, light interacts with particles suspended in the water within the camera's \gls{fov}.
Backscatter is light reflected back towards the camera by these particles, which brightens the image unevenly across
the frame. This strains the camera's dynamic range, the disparity between the brightest and darkest light a sensor can
record in a single image \cite{treibitzActivePolarizationDescattering2009}. Forward scatter is light from the object
that is slightly deflected on its way to the camera, blurring fine detail. Both effects are likely to be a problem in the \gls{miz}, due to frazil crystals into the upper water column
\cite{dobleWeddellPancake2003,shenConceptualModelPancakeice2001} and marine snow\footnote{Marine snow: small clumps of organic material that drift down through the water, seen as white specks.} \cite{alldredgeMarineSnow1988}.

Lighting is a further challenge. The intensity of light reaching the underside
of the ice depends on the density of overlying snow, and on ice
concentration \cite{horvatEvidencePhytoplanktonBlooms2022}.
Arndt et al.~\cite{arndtInfluenceSnowDepth2017} measured irradiance\footnote{Irradiance: The
radiant power received per unit area, here expressed as a
percentage of the light at the surface.} below Weddell Sea pack
ice at under 0.1\,\%. This is too dim for imaging \cite{katleinNewRemotelyOperated2017}, so artificial lighting
is likely needed in this application. This introduces further problems, including backscatter,
brightness falloff, and uneven illumination
\cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}. Pancake ice adds further
complications, as gaps between pancakes let bright light through,
creating contrast that may exceed the camera's dynamic range.

Furthermore, the ice underside also offers little texture for feature-based
reconstruction, which rely on  distinctive points, such as corners and blobs, and match them between images,
rejecting points with low contrast \cite{lowe2004}. A smooth, uniform surface gives few reliable matches.
Surfaces are often smooth
\cite{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989}, and features such as air
bubbles are small, low in contrast and repetitive.
Zhao et al.~\cite{zhaoModifyingAffordableROV2021} deployed an upward-looking
stereo camera on a BlueROV2
beneath freshwater ice in Michigan. Despite adequate light,
visual odometry\footnote{Visual odometry: Estimating a vehicle's motion by tracking
how features in consecutive images shift.} failed due to
 ``ubiquitous and similar'' ice.


Characteristics of the hardware can further contribute to image degradation.
Underwater cameras view the scene through a transparent window, or
\textit{port}, in a waterproof housing. The port may be flat or dome-shaped,
and light bends at the water-glass-air interface
\cite{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}.
Treibitz et al.~\cite{treibitzFlatRefractiveGeometry2012} showed that a camera
behind a flat port no longer has a single viewpoint, and so the
standard pinhole model produces distance-dependent errors (see
\autoref{subsec:ch3.underwater_calib}).


Together, these effects make clean, geometrically accurate images
difficult to capture. This places heavy emphasis on calibration and
image correction. Cameras must be optically corrected or well
calibrated \cite{shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}, and
lighting and distance need careful attention
\cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990}. The subsequent section
reviews existing three-dimensional reconstruction techniques and describe how these challenges are mitigated.

\cite{massot-camposOpticalSensorsMethods2015,huOverviewUnderwater3D2023}, for reasons explained in \autoref{sec:ch3.stereo_geometry}.
Publicly available material on three-dimensional reconstruction beneath ice is limited.
Stereo reconstruction is nonetheless widely used elsewhere underwater, including ship-hull inspection
\cite{negahdaripourROVStereovisionSystem2006}, seafloor mapping

\cite{friedmanMultiScaleMeasuresRugosity2012,pizarroSimpleFastRepeatable2017} and archaeology

Summarising \autoref{tab:ch2.stereo_comparison}, most systems use a downward-looking stereo pair with a 7 to 9\,cm baseline at an distance from the target of approximately 2\,m, 
and are fitted  with strobes to reduce backscatter 
\cite{johnson-robersonGenerationVisualizationLargescale2010,johnson-robersonHighResolutionUnderwaterRobotic2017}. 

Processing is typically done offline after data collection, and involves combining stereo matching, \gls{slam} or bundle adjustment to correct 
drift, and mesh fusion. Processing times range from roughly the duration of the mission 
\cite{johnson-robersonGenerationVisualizationLargescale2010} to 13.6\,h for 360{,}000 images 
\cite{johnson-robersonHighResolutionUnderwaterRobotic2017}. Only 
Negahdaripour and Firoozfam~\cite{negahdaripourROVStereovisionSystem2006} operated in real time.

\section{Critique of existing literature}
\label{sec:ch2.critique}
The underside of Antarctic pack and pancake ice remains largely unmapped at centimetre scale.
At the time of this project, no three-dimensional reconstructions of the underside of pancake ice have been published.
This is a significant gap, as the underside forms the interface between the ice and the ocean.
Its shape controls drag and heat exchange with the ocean, and it hosts sea-ice algae,
the main primary producers when the open water is least productive \cite{lizotteContributionsSeaIce2001}.
Direct measurements of the underside are needed to improve models of these processes.

Existing methods do not fill this gap. Satellites, aircraft and ships infer the ice thickness from above, but cannot see the shape of the underside.
Multibeam sonar has provided the most detailed underside maps in the Antarctic to date, but at about 0.5\,m horizontal
resolution it cannot resolve the centimetre-scale structure of pancake ice \cite{williamsThickDeformedAntarctic2015}.
Under-ice cameras could capture this detail, yet they are mostly used for piloting and visual observation
\cite{katleinNewRemotelyOperated2017}. The only camera-based reconstruction of the Antarctic ice underside was made on
land-fast ice \cite{cimoliUnderIceHyperspectralRGB2019}.

The literature also shows why camera-based reconstruction beneath pancake ice is challenging. The light under the ice is
too dim for imaging, so artificial lighting is needed \cite{arndtInfluenceSnowDepth2017,katleinNewRemotelyOperated2017},
while gaps between pancakes let through bright light that may exceed the camera's dynamic range.
Backscatter and forward scatter reduce contrast and blur fine detail
\cite{jaffeComputerModelingDesign1990,treibitzActivePolarizationDescattering2009}, attenuation and refraction distort the
images \cite{akkaynakWhatSpaceAttenuation2017,shortisCalibrationTechniquesAccurate2015}, and the smooth underside offers
little texture for feature matching \cite{langeDevelopmentSeaIce1989,lowe2004}. Overhangs formed by rafting
(\autoref{fig:ch2.pancake_deck}) may also hide parts of the surface from a single viewpoint, so several views must be
combined. Existing underwater stereo systems were developed for the seafloor, coral reefs, ship hulls and archaeological
sites, and most process their data offline (\autoref{tab:ch2.stereo_comparison}).

The literature therefore highlights the importance of careful calibration and of compensating for the underwater
environment before a reconstruction can be trusted. These considerations are taken into account in the following chapters.
