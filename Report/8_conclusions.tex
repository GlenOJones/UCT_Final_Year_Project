\label{ch:conclusions}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------

This project carried out an initial proof of concept for underwater three-dimensional stereo reconstruction, using
the BlueROV2 platform. The aim was to generate a dataset and to analyse the surface reconstruction of
known targets, as a first step towards reconstructing the underside of Antarctic sea ice.
% Lit review summary
The report began by reviewing the existing literature in \hyperref[ch:review]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:review}}. The role of the Southern Ocean
and its sea ice in absorbing anthropogenic heat and CO\textsubscript{2} and in the ecosystem was examined, and the
formation of pancake ice by wave action within the \gls{miz} was described. The shortcomings of satellite and
above-ice observations showed the need to observe from below the ice. Previous sonar and optical measurements,
the difficulties of underwater optics and existing underwater reconstructions were then reviewed.

% Theory summary
The theory in \hyperref[ch:theory]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:theory}} built the pinhole camera model from first principles, then covered
calibration, stereo depth maps, and posing and fusing frames into a three-dimensional reconstruction.

% Hardware and software explanation
The hardware and software were described in \hyperref[ch:design]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:design}}. Dropouts in the stereo camera feeds were
traced to USB bandwidth on the \gls{rpi} and fixed by upgrading from an \gls{rpi}~4 to an \gls{rpi}~5.
\hyperref[sec:testplatform]{Section~\ref*{sec:testplatform}} described the test rig and calibration board used to record the dataset. This dataset is
a significant product of the research, as it contains further recordings that future students can use.

% Calibration summary, what was done, what was concluded
The experimental work began with underwater stereo calibration in \hyperref[ch:calibration]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:calibration}}. Merging the
greyscale and colour channel detections raised the AprilTag detection rate from 0.354 to 0.432, and tuning the
OpenCV detector kept 75\,\% more tags. Five calibration approaches, two based on in-air calibration and three
fitted to underwater images, were then tested on repeated train and held-out splits. Calibrating with underwater
images was crucial for accuracy across the full field of view. The underwater Brown-Conrady model was
selected, with a held-out reprojection error of 0.29\,px against 8.6\,px for the in-air calibration and 1.9\,px for
Pinax, and it measured tags on an independent target to within about 2\,mm (1.5\,\%). An axial shift model showed
that the Aquagon optics follow the single-viewpoint approximation, so the standard pinhole model could be used.

% 3D Reconstruction
Three-dimensional surfaces were then generated from the calibrated stereo pairs in \hyperref[ch:reconstruction]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:reconstruction}}.
For depth, the classical \gls{sgbm} matcher was compared with the learnt FoundationStereo and AquaStereo models.
FoundationStereo gave the highest coverage in shade and was the only matcher to keep the full ice shape in direct
sunlight. For pose, COLMAP was the most accurate in shade, but its poses jumped in sunlight, where only \gls{icp}
with FoundationStereo depth tracked the camera throughout. For fusion, \gls{tsdf} with FoundationStereo gave the
most complete and accurate pyramids and the cleanest ice surfaces. On the egg carton the learnt model placed a
smooth surface across a partly hidden curved region, so reconstructions of real sea ice should be checked against
COLMAP. Fewer frames had little effect on the surfaces facing the camera but degraded the side walls. The final
pipeline uses COLMAP for pose, FoundationStereo for depth and \gls{tsdf} for fusion, and takes around 7\,s per frame.

This project successfully demonstrated an initial test of underwater three-dimensional stereo reconstruction using
the BlueROV2, producing a thorough dataset and selecting the best method for each stage of the pipeline. It was not
designed to be ready for full deployment, but it serves as a solid foundation and proof of concept, and further work
is discussed in the following chapter.
