\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusions}
%---------------------------------------------------------------------
%
% Add references to full chaptes. I e in chapter on , or (Chapter 2)

This project set out to research and carry out an initial proof of concept for underwater three-dimensional
stereo reconstruction, using the \gls{uct} BlueROV2 platform. The aim was to generate a dataset and to analyse the
surface reconstruction of known targets, as a first step towards assessing the feasibility of reconstructing the
underside of Antarctic sea ice.

% Lit review summary
The report began by reviewing the existing literature (\hyperref[ch:review]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:review}}). The role of the Southern Ocean
and its sea ice in absorbing anthropogenic heat and CO\textsubscript{2}, and the role of sea ice in the ecosystem,
were examined. The formation of pancake ice within the \gls{miz} was then described, showing how wave action breaks
forming ice into pancake-like disks. The shortcomings of satellite and above-ice observations were identified,
showing the need to observe from below the ice. Previous sonar and optical measurements in the region were
summarised and compared. Finally, the difficulties of optical observation underwater were explained, and
existing underwater reconstructions from different applications were examined.

% Theory summary
Building on this research and its associated challenges, the underlying theory was developed in
\hyperref[ch:theory]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:theory}}. The pinhole camera model was built up from first principles, followed by camera
calibration, how a stereo pair generates depth maps, and how the resulting frames are posed and fused into a three-dimensional
reconstruction.

% Hardware and software explanation
The hardware and software used for data collection and processing were then described in
\hyperref[ch:design]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:design}}. This highlighted the dropouts in the stereo camera feeds, which were traced to USB
bandwidth on the \gls{rpi} and fixed by upgrading it from an \gls{rpi}~4 to an \gls{rpi}~5.

\hyperref[ch:dataset]{Section~\ref*{ch:dataset}} described how the test rig and calibration board were designed, built and used to
record the dataset for this report. This dataset is a significant product of the research, as it contains further
recordings beyond the three datasets used here. It can be used by future students or researchers, either to
extend this work or for other tests.

% Calibration summary, what was done, what was concluded
The experimental work began with underwater stereo calibration (\hyperref[ch:calibration]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:calibration}}). AprilTag detection
was first improved by merging the detections from the greyscale and colour channels, which raised the detection
rate from 0.354 to 0.432, and then by tuning the OpenCV detector, which kept 75\,\% more tags.
With enough tags detected for reliable calibration, five approaches to underwater calibration were
tested: two based on in-air calibration and three fitted to underwater images. Repeated train and held-out splits
were used to reduce bias in the evaluation. Calibrating with underwater images was found to be crucial for
preserving accuracy across the full field of view. The underwater Brown-Conrady model was selected, with a
held-out reprojection error of 0.29\,px, against 8.6\,px for the in-air calibration and 1.9\,px for Pinax, and it
measured tags on an independent target to within about 2\,mm (1.5\,\%). Testing an axial shift model further
showed that the Aquagon optics follow the single-viewpoint approximation, so the underwater images could be
processed with the standard pinhole camera model.

% 3D Reconstruction
The next stage generated three-dimensional surfaces from the calibrated stereo pairs (\hyperref[ch:reconstruction]{Chapter~\ref*{ch:reconstruction}}). This was
split into depth map generation, estimating each frame's pose, and fusing the frames into a three-dimensional point cloud. For
depth map generation, a classical matcher (\gls{sgbm}) was compared with two learnt models, FoundationStereo and
AquaStereo. FoundationStereo gave the highest coverage on the ice targets in shade and was the only matcher to
keep the full ice shape in direct sunlight, where most of the \gls{sgbm} depth was speckle. For pose estimation, COLMAP
produced the most accurate trajectories in shade, but in sunlight its poses jumped between frames and left. \Gls{icp} with
FoundationStereo depth maps was the second most accurate in shade, and the only method to track the camera
throughout the sunlit pass. Frame fusion then compared COLMAP's PatchMatch stereo with \gls{tsdf} fusion of both the
FoundationStereo and the tuned \gls{sgbm} depth maps. \Gls{tsdf} with FoundationStereo performed best overall, giving the most
complete and accurate reconstructions of both pyramids and the cleanest surfaces on the ice targets. However, on
the egg carton, the learnt model placed a smooth surface across a partly hidden curved region, resulting in a high
error. It was therefore recommended that reconstructions of real sea ice be compared against COLMAP, to check how
often this occurs. Finally, the full pipeline was tested with reduced numbers of frames, and the run times were
measured. Reducing the number of frames had little impact on the surfaces facing the camera, but the side walls,
which are only seen in a few frames, became degraded. The final pipeline uses COLMAP for pose, FoundationStereo for
depth and \gls{tsdf} for fusion, and takes around 7\,s per frame.

This project successfully demonstrated an initial test of underwater three-dimensional stereo reconstruction using the BlueROV2.
A test rig was manufactured and a thorough dataset was generated. Different implementations were tested for the
calibration, depth generation, pose estimation and frame fusion stages of the pipeline, and the best method for
each stage was selected. Specific failure cases were identified for each method, and the performance of the full
pipeline was analysed. The project was not designed to be ready for full deployment, but it serves as a solid
foundation and proof of concept. Furute work discussed in the following chapter
